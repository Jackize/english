### Reading passage

**The Measurement Trap**

It is a familiar complaint that modern institutions are obsessed with numbers. Hospitals count waiting times, schools publish exam results, police forces report crime figures, and universities rank themselves by citation counts. The complaint is often dismissed as nostalgia: what alternative is there to measurement, other than prejudice and anecdote? Yet the most interesting critics of metrics do not reject measurement in principle. They argue that a particular kind of measurement — a single, high-stakes indicator used to judge a complex activity — tends to produce the very behaviour it is meant to detect.

The mechanism was described long before it acquired a name. When a measure becomes a target, people adapt to the measure rather than to the underlying goal. A hospital told to reduce waiting times can leave patients in ambulances outside the emergency department, thereby stopping the clock. A school judged by exam passes can focus on borderline students and neglect those who are already secure. A police force judged by arrest numbers can pursue easy arrests rather than serious crimes. In each case, the indicator is not falsified; it is satisfied in a way that defeats its purpose. The result is not mere cheating but a subtle reorganisation of priorities around what can be counted.

Defenders of metrics reply that these are problems of implementation, not of measurement itself. If an indicator is well designed, resistant to gaming, and combined with professional judgement, it can improve accountability. This is true as far as it goes. Patients benefit when waiting times are public; parents benefit when schools are transparent; citizens benefit when police data are open. The question is not whether to measure but how to prevent a measure from becoming a proxy for the whole good.

One answer is to use multiple indicators, so that gaming one distorts another. A hospital might report waiting times, infection rates, readmission rates and patient experience. A university might report graduation rates, employment outcomes and research quality. Yet multiplicity is not a cure-all. Indicators can be correlated, so that gaming one improves the others; or they can be combined into a single index whose weights are hidden. The more complex the dashboard, the easier it is for an institution to present the best face and bury the rest.

A deeper problem is that some goods are intrinsically difficult to quantify. The quality of a doctor’s attention, the seriousness of a teacher’s interest in a struggling pupil, the patience of a social worker — these are not easily captured by a number. They may be visible to colleagues and service users, but they resist aggregation. When such goods are excluded from measurement, they tend to become invisible in budgets and promotions. The indicator does not merely describe reality; it helps decide what counts as real.

This is why the most persuasive critics advocate a division of labour. Measurement should be used for **diagnosis**, not judgement: to reveal patterns, prompt questions and identify outliers. Judgement should remain with people who know the context and can weigh what the numbers omit. In practice, this means treating metrics as evidence to be interpreted, not as verdicts to be applied. It also means protecting spaces where professionals can discuss cases without the pressure of a public score.

The objection that this is soft-minded is weaker than it appears. Judgement without measurement is vulnerable to bias; measurement without judgement is vulnerable to distortion. The choice is not between numbers and intuition but between different combinations of the two. The question is who gets to interpret, on what evidence, and with what right of reply. These are political questions, not technical ones, and they cannot be settled by improving the dashboard.

None of this implies that metrics are sinister. Many are useful, and some are indispensable. But the fantasy of a single number that captures a complex good is not a neutral mistake. It shapes behaviour, distributes attention and decides whose work is visible. A society that measures only what it can count will eventually count only what it can see.

### Reading passage

**The Afterlife of Ruins**

Every restored building carries a small lie. The lie is not the new stone, which is usually honest about being new, but the suggestion that the building has survived unchanged. A medieval church with a Victorian roof, a Roman wall rebuilt in the 1930s, a “traditional” village constructed for tourists in the 1990s: each is a palimpsest, a surface written over many times. Conservationists know this. Visitors often do not. The gap between the two kinds of knowledge is where the heritage industry does much of its work.

The romantic view of ruins is that decay is eloquent. A broken arch, open to the sky, is said to speak of time, loss and the vanity of human ambition. This is a powerful aesthetic, and it is not wrong. But it is also a convenience. A ruin that is too dangerous to enter can be admired from a distance; a ruin that is too expensive to repair can be presented as deliberately preserved. Decay, in other words, can be a budget line dressed as a philosophy.

Consider the familiar phrase “preserved for future generations”. It sounds selfless, and sometimes it is. But it also postpones the question of whose future is being imagined. A village cleared of its residents so that tourists can admire its “authentic” architecture has been preserved for a future generation of visitors, not for the people who once lived there. The building survives; the community that gave it meaning does not. The result may be visually convincing and socially hollow.

This is not to say that every restoration is a disguise. Some interventions are modest, reversible and clearly labelled. The best conservation work often does less than visitors expect: it stabilises a structure, repairs a roof, removes a harmful addition, and then stops. Its restraint is a form of honesty. The problem arises when restraint is replaced by spectacle — when the past is not conserved but staged, and the staging is presented as recovery.

Tourism intensifies the pressure. A place that becomes famous for being unspoiled rarely stays unspoiled for long. The first visitors bring money; the second bring hotels; the third bring the infrastructure that the first visitors came to escape. Locals may welcome the income, and it would be condescending to assume they cannot decide for themselves. But the incentives are not neutral. The more a place performs its own past, the more it depends on the performance, and the harder it becomes to live in the present.

There is an irony here that the heritage industry rarely acknowledges. The search for authenticity often produces its opposite: a carefully managed surface that satisfies the visitor’s expectation of what the past should look like. The visitor photographs the empty street, the wooden shutters, the woman in traditional dress, and goes home believing they have seen something older than themselves. What they have seen is a contemporary product, designed to look old. This is not necessarily fraud. It is a transaction, and both sides may understand it. But it is not the past.

What would a more honest heritage practice look like? It might begin by admitting that conservation is always a choice about what to value. It might display the evidence of change rather than conceal it: the different stones, the later windows, the marks of repair. It might consult the people who live there, not only the people who visit. And it might accept that some things cannot be saved, or should not be saved at the expense of the living.

None of this requires us to stop caring about old buildings. It requires us to be less sentimental about them. A building is not valuable because it is old, but because of what it enables: memory, shelter, collective life, a sense of continuity. When conservation protects the shell and destroys the life inside, it has confused the monument with the thing it was meant to commemorate.

### Before you read

1. Khi tự động hóa phát triển, nghề thủ công còn chỗ không?
2. “Craft” có phải chỉ là đồ làm tay không?

### Reading passage

**Four Perspectives on Craft in an Automated Economy**

**A. The economist**

Craft production will survive, but as a niche rather than a model. The reason is simple: automation raises output per hour, and consumers generally prefer cheaper goods when quality is adequate. Handmade furniture, bespoke clothing and artisanal food will remain attractive to a minority willing to pay for distinction, but they cannot furnish a mass market. Policies that protect craft employment through subsidies tend to preserve inefficiency and delay the transition to higher-productivity sectors. The sensible response is not to romanticise the workshop but to help workers acquire transferable skills, so that they can move into design, maintenance or small-scale entrepreneurship. Craft will not disappear; it will become a luxury segment with a small, stable workforce.

**B. The sociologist**

The economist’s account misses what work means to the people who do it. Craft is not merely a production method; it is a relationship between a person, a material and a standard. The craftsman knows when a joint is tight, when a surface is true, when a piece is finished. That knowledge is embodied and slow to acquire, and it gives work a shape that automated production often lacks. When a task is broken into fragments and controlled by software, the worker may become more productive but less able to exercise judgement. This is not nostalgia; it is an argument about the conditions of meaningful work. Societies should ask not only how much is produced but whether the work enables people to develop and take pride in what they do.

**C. The designer**

The debate is framed as a choice between hands and machines, and that framing is obsolete. Digital tools allow small workshops to produce customised goods at a scale that was impossible a generation ago. A furniture maker can use software to model a chair, a computer-controlled cutter to shape components, and hand-finishing to give the surface its character. The result is neither pure craft nor mass production but a hybrid. This hybrid requires new skills: not only the ability to use a chisel but also the ability to program a machine, understand materials and explain a design to a client. The craftsman of the future may be someone who moves fluently between code and grain.

**D. The historian**

The idea of a pure, unchanging craft tradition is a modern invention. Guilds regulated production, technologies travelled along trade routes, and workshops adopted new tools whenever they were useful. What we now call “traditional” methods are often the residue of earlier innovations. This matters because it undercuts the assumption that automation is an alien force threatening an authentic past. Crafts have always adapted or disappeared. The interesting question is not how to preserve a fixed repertoire but how to keep the capacity for repair, adaptation and transmission alive. A society that treats craft as a museum piece may preserve objects while losing the knowledge needed to maintain them.

### Reading passage

**The Repair Paradox**

Repair is one of the simplest environmental acts available to a household. Extending the life of a kettle, a phone or a coat saves the materials and energy that would otherwise go into a replacement. It also keeps money in the local economy and preserves skills that are otherwise lost. On these grounds, governments and campaigners increasingly urge people to repair rather than replace. Yet the advice runs against the economic logic of the market. In many cases, a new item costs less than a repair, and the difference is not a matter of individual laziness. It is designed into the product and reinforced by the way prices are set.

The design of modern goods often makes repair difficult by intention. Batteries are sealed inside cases, components are glued rather than screwed, and specialist tools are needed to open a device. Software may refuse to recognise a replacement part. Manufacturers defend these choices on grounds of safety and intellectual property: a device that is easy to open may be dangerous, and a design that can be copied may undermine investment. Critics reply that these arguments are often pretexts. A sealed battery may be safer for the manufacturer’s warranty, but it also ensures that the device is replaced rather than repaired.

Right-to-repair legislation attempts to shift the balance. Such laws typically require manufacturers to provide spare parts, tools and manuals, and sometimes access to software needed to pair a new component. The aim is not to force people to repair, but to make repair possible. Opposition comes from firms that argue that compliance is costly and that open access may compromise security. The dispute is therefore not only about consumer convenience; it is about who controls the knowledge embedded in a product.

Repair cafés have emerged as a practical response. Volunteers help people fix small appliances, clothes and bicycles, often for free. The benefits go beyond the object: repair cafés create social contact, reduce waste and transfer skills between generations. A retired engineer may show a teenager how to solder; a neighbour may learn how to replace a zip. Yet volunteers are the first to admit that cafés cannot substitute for systemic change. They handle a tiny fraction of discarded goods, and they cannot compel manufacturers to design differently.

The deeper problem is that repair is treated as a private virtue rather than a public infrastructure. A household that repairs is praised, but the systems that make repair possible — spare parts, manuals, skilled technicians, standards — are underfunded. Governments can change this by requiring products to be designed for repairability, extending warranties, shifting taxes from labour to materials, and setting common standards for parts. None of these measures is glamorous, but together they alter the default.

There is also a cultural dimension. A society that equates newness with progress may view repair as a sign of poverty or failure. Campaigners try to reverse this by presenting repair as a form of resistance to throwaway culture. The framing is powerful, but it can also be misleading. Repair is not simply an act of individual virtue, and it is not a complete solution to overconsumption. It is a practice that challenges the assumption that ownership means replacing rather than maintaining. In that sense, it is both practical and political: it asks who has the right to open a product, who possesses the knowledge to fix it, and what kind of economy makes throwing things away the rational choice.

### Reading passage

**The Open Science Paradox**

Open science is usually presented as an unqualified good. Research funded by the public should be publicly available; data should be shared so that findings can be checked; code should be open so that results can be reproduced. These principles have changed academic life. Journals that once charged readers now charge authors, and a growing number of papers are free to read. Data repositories allow researchers to inspect claims that were once taken on trust. In fields such as genomics and climate science, shared datasets have enabled discoveries that no single laboratory could have made alone.

Yet the movement has produced a paradox. The more research becomes open, the more valuable it becomes to be able to use it — and the more unequal the ability to do so. A well-funded laboratory can afford the fees that many journals charge for open-access publication. It can employ data managers, pay for computing time and navigate the legal and ethical requirements that accompany sensitive data. A researcher in a poorly funded institution may be able to read the same paper but not to publish in the same venue, nor to reuse the data in a meaningful way. Openness, in other words, can expose a gap that it does not close.

The problem is not only money. Open data requires infrastructure: storage, metadata, version control and long-term maintenance. A dataset that is dumped online without documentation is technically open but practically useless. The researchers who create such infrastructure are often invisible in citation metrics, and their work is rarely rewarded by promotion committees. Meanwhile, the pressure to share can encourage superficial compliance: datasets are released in formats that satisfy a funder’s checklist but cannot be reused. The result is a growing archive of data that is open in name and closed in practice.

There is also an ethical tension. Some data cannot be fully open without exposing individuals or communities. Medical records, genetic information and data from vulnerable populations require controlled access. The debate is sometimes framed as a conflict between openness and privacy, but the more difficult question is who controls the conditions of access. A community that contributes data may be asked to accept that the data will be used by researchers it has never met, in projects it did not design, with benefits that may never return to it. Consent, in such cases, is not a single moment but an ongoing relationship.

Publishers have adapted in ways that complicate the picture. Author-pays models have expanded access for readers, but they have also created incentives to publish more, especially in journals with lower rejection rates. The result can be a flood of low-quality papers, which makes it harder for readers to identify reliable work. At the same time, the largest publishers have increased their share of the market, because they can offer the prestige and technical systems that authors need. Open access has not necessarily democratised publishing; it has changed who pays.

Critics of the movement are often accused of defending the old order. Some are. But the strongest critique does not reject openness; it asks what openness is for. If the goal is to make knowledge available, then access is necessary but not sufficient. People also need the time, skills, equipment and freedom to use it. If the goal is to improve the reliability of research, then transparency is necessary but not sufficient; incentives must reward careful work, replication and the unglamorous labour of maintenance. If the goal is to reduce inequality, then making outputs free while leaving inputs expensive may achieve little.

A more defensible model would treat openness as a set of graduated commitments rather than a binary. Raw data might be shared under controlled conditions; metadata and protocols could be open by default; publications could be free to read and affordable to publish. Funders could pay for infrastructure rather than only for articles, and promotion criteria could recognise data curation, replication and peer review. Such reforms are less exciting than a manifesto, but they address the actual bottlenecks.

The deepest paradox is that open science depends on resources that are not themselves open. Trust, time, training and institutions are not downloaded with a dataset. A researcher who lacks a stable electricity supply cannot benefit from a free PDF. The movement’s future therefore depends less on declaring information free than on building the conditions under which people can use it. Openness is a means, not an end; and a means that is celebrated without being funded can become another way of reproducing the inequalities it was meant to overcome.
