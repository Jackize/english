# C1 Reading — Dense Texts, Inference & Speed

**Level:** C1 (CEFR) · **IELTS Academic:** 7.5–8.0 · **Số unit:** 6  
**Trọng tâm:** Dense academic text, inference/implication, writer's purpose and tone, matching information, abstract summary completion, vocabulary in context, complex sentence decoding, multiple matching, speed  
**Ngôn ngữ:** hướng dẫn tiếng Việt; bài đọc, câu hỏi và đáp án tiếng Anh

> Ở C1, khoảng cách giữa 7.0 và 8.0 không nằm ở việc đọc nhanh hơn, mà ở chỗ **bạn có tin vào bằng chứng hay không**. Người đọc 7.0 thường chọn đáp án "nghe hợp lý"; người đọc 8.0 chỉ chọn khi chỉ ra được câu văn làm chỗ dựa và giải thích được vì sao ba đáp án kia sai. Tài liệu này luyện đúng thói quen đó.

## 0. Giới thiệu & cách học

### 0.1 Hồ sơ người đọc C1

Ở band 7.5–8.0, bài đọc Academic thường có 2.150–2.750 từ cho ba passage, nhiều câu dài 35–60 từ, mật độ trừu tượng cao và lập luận có ngoại lệ. Bạn không cần hiểu từng từ, nhưng phải:

- nhận ra **chức năng của từng đoạn** (giải thích, phản biện, đưa ví dụ, giới hạn, kết luận);
- phân biệt **điều được nói**, **điều được ngụ ý** và **điều không được nói**;
- theo dõi **phạm vi** của câu (*most, some, often, in part, unless*) và **mức độ chắc chắn** (*may, suggests, is likely to*);
- xử lý câu phức bằng cách tách mệnh đề chính, mệnh đề phụ và cụm danh từ trừu tượng;
- giữ tốc độ ổn định: 17–18 phút cho một passage 900–1.100 từ, kể cả lúc chuyển câu hỏi.

### 0.2 Sau 6 unit, bạn có thể

- Đọc passage học thuật dày đặc 900–1.050 từ trong 17–18 phút và trả lời 10–13 câu.
- Suy luận một bước từ bằng chứng, không suy diễn hai bước hay dùng kiến thức ngoài bài.
- Nhận diện mục đích, thái độ và giọng điệu của người viết qua lựa chọn từ và cấu trúc câu.
- Làm Matching Information, Summary Completion với từ trừu tượng, Vocabulary in Context và Multiple Matching.
- Giải mã câu phức, câu có phủ định kép, câu điều kiện và câu có mệnh đề quan hệ lồng nhau.
- Quản lý thời gian dưới áp lực và ghi lỗi theo một hệ mã thống nhất.

### 0.3 Thời gian: 17–18 phút mỗi passage

| Giai đoạn | Thời gian | Việc cần làm |
|---|---:|---|
| Survey | 1,5–2 phút | Đọc tiêu đề, câu đầu mỗi đoạn, nhãn A–F/G, loại câu hỏi |
| Predict | 1,5 phút | Đọc câu hỏi, gạch chân giới hạn từ và từ khóa logic; đoán nghĩa trước khi xem options |
| Locate & answer | 11–12 phút | Tìm bằng chứng, viết nhãn đoạn bên cạnh đáp án |
| Verify | 2 phút | Kiểm tra phạm vi, thì, số từ, numbering |
| Review | 1 phút | Chỉ xem lại câu để trống; không đổi đáp án nếu không có bằng chứng mới |

Trong full test 60 phút cho 40 câu, đừng chia đều 20 phút/passage. Passage 1 nên xong trong 16–17 phút, passage 2 trong 18 phút, passage 3 trong 20–21 phút và để 3–4 phút cuối kiểm tra. Mục tiêu 7.5–8.0 tương đương khoảng **33–36/40**.

### 0.4 Phương pháp dựa trên bằng chứng

Mỗi đáp án phải trả lời được ba câu hỏi:

1. **Evidence ở đâu?** Ghi nhãn đoạn và số câu (ví dụ: ¶C, câu 3).
2. **Paraphrase là gì?** Từ nào trong bài tương ứng với từ nào trong câu hỏi?
3. **Vì sao ba đáp án kia sai?** Sai vì trái bài, vì quá rộng/hẹp, vì không được nhắc đến, hay vì đúng nhưng không trả lời câu hỏi?

Quy tắc: **No evidence, no mark.** Nếu phải suy luận hai bước, đáp án gần như chắc chắn sai. Nếu một đáp án đúng nhưng không giải thích được lỗi của các đáp án còn lại, hãy đọc lại câu hỏi — bạn có thể đang trả lời sai loại câu.

### 0.8 Cách học một unit

1. Đọc câu hỏi trước, không đọc options ngay: dự đoán câu trả lời bằng lời của bạn.
2. Skim passage 2 phút; viết 2–4 từ tóm tắt chức năng mỗi đoạn ra lề.
3. Làm bài đúng 17–18 phút. Câu nào chưa chắc, đánh dấu và quay lại sau.
4. Chấm bằng answer key; với mỗi câu sai, viết lại evidence và lý do đáp án đúng.
5. Học 10 từ trong bảng; đặt câu với collocation, không học từ đơn.
6. Sau 48 giờ, làm lại passage mà không xem đáp án. Mục tiêu: giải thích được cả 4 options.

## Unit 1 — Dense Academic Text, Inference & Implication

**Mục tiêu:** Đọc câu dày đặc; phân biệt stated / implied / not given; suy luận đúng một bước.  
**Độ dài:** ~950 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 12 · **Thời gian:** 18 phút.

### Reading passage

**The Work That Leaves No Trace**

**A.** At six o'clock on a winter morning, a woman in her fifties is helping her father dress. She has done this every weekday for two years. The work requires judgement, patience and a knowledge of his moods that no training course could supply. It is also, in the language of economics, invisible. It appears in no national account, contributes nothing to gross domestic product and is recorded nowhere in the labour statistics. Economists call it unpaid care: the cooking, cleaning, nursing, supervising and ferrying that households perform without payment. The omission is not a judgement that such work is unimportant. It follows from what the statistical system was built to measure. National accounts were designed to track transactions, and an activity can be indispensable while leaving no transaction behind. It is work that would still need doing if no one were counting. The omission is a choice with consequences.

**B.** The boundary is drawn by a convention rather than by nature. The System of National Accounts, the international rulebook that governments follow, includes the rent that owner-occupiers would pay to live in their own homes; statisticians impute a value even though no money changes hands. It excludes the cleaning, cooking and child care that make those homes livable. If a family hires a cleaner, GDP rises. If the same family cleans its own house, GDP does not move. A transaction crossing the garden fence changes the number. This is sometimes described as a conspiracy against women, but the history is less dramatic. The framework took shape in the mid-twentieth century, when market output was treated as a reasonable proxy for production and when the tools for valuing household services did not exist. It was a convention, not a conspiracy — which is precisely why it has been so hard to change. The distinction is not trivial: it determines whether a society appears productive when it merely shifts work across the boundary.

**C.** Time-use surveys reveal the scale of what the convention hides. Respondents keep diaries, recording primary activities such as cooking alongside secondary ones such as watching a child, and researchers convert the minutes into estimates of value. Cross-national studies consistently find that women perform two to three times as much unpaid care as men, though the gap varies with employment, migration and the availability of public services. The estimates are imperfect. People forget what they did, round their hours, and report simultaneous activities inconsistently. Even so, conservative valuations place unpaid care at 20–40 per cent of measured GDP in many economies — a range wide enough to invite disagreement but too large to dismiss. The lower figure alone would exceed the output of agriculture in most high-income countries. The range is wide because researchers disagree about what counts as care and how to price it, not because the phenomenon is small.

**D.** The consequences of this blindness are not merely statistical. When governments design childcare policy, they assume that families will absorb whatever gap remains between a parent's working hours and a school's opening hours. When budgets are cut, the same assumption reappears: care will be provided by someone, and that someone is usually a woman. The result is a labour market that depends on unpaid work while pretending not to, and pension systems that reward paid employment across a lifetime during which unpaid work has interrupted it. It is often said that the economy is a camera with a frame. What lies outside the frame is not unreal; it is simply unrecorded. Unpaid care is not a footnote to the paid economy. It is a subsidy that makes the paid economy possible. Once the subsidy is named, apparently technical decisions about tax, childcare and pensions become decisions about who will absorb the cost of reproducing the workforce.

**E.** Some statisticians have tried to bring care inside the frame through satellite accounts: separate ledgers that sit alongside GDP rather than replacing it. The technical problem is valuation. The replacement-cost approach asks what it would cost to hire someone to do the work. The opportunity-cost approach asks what the carer could otherwise have earned. Neither is neutral. Opportunity cost values a lawyer's hour more than a cleaner's, so an identical act of care appears more valuable when performed by a high earner. Replacement cost may understate the skill involved and, by setting a wage, can make care look like a service that can be cut when budgets tighten. Inclusion is an improvement on invisibility, but it does not remove the assumptions; it merely makes them visible.

**F.** The debate about measurement is therefore also a debate about power. A society that counts only what is bought will govern as though only buyers and sellers exist. Yet the answer is not to price everything. Some advocates conclude that family carers should be paid a wage; others worry that turning care into a job would formalise what many people value precisely because it is not a job. Both positions are defensible, and the passage does not settle them. What it does suggest is that refusing to measure care is not a neutral act either. The unmeasured are easily treated as though they had no claim on public resources. The question is not whether to count care, but who decides how, and for what purpose. That is a democratic question as much as an economic one.

**9.** National accounts include an estimated rent for homes occupied by their owners.

**11.** The writer recommends that the state should pay family carers a wage.

### Answer key

1. **B** — ¶A: the care is *"indispensable while leaving no transaction behind"*. The opening is an example of statistical invisibility, not of physical exhaustion (A), hiring (C) or blame (D).
2. **A** — ¶B: hiring a cleaner raises GDP; cleaning your own house does not. B reverses the point about value; C invents a tax rule; D contradicts the passage's central claim.
3. **D** — ¶B: *"It was a convention, not a conspiracy"* and the framework was a practical product of its time, not a plan against women. A is too absolute (people did design the framework); B is false (disagreement persists); C is contradicted by ¶E.
4. **C** — ¶C: the surveys reveal scale but *"estimates are imperfect"*. A and B are extremes; D is not mentioned.
5. **A** — ¶D: the frame determines what is recorded; the unrecorded is *"not unreal"*. B accuses statisticians of fraud; C misreads a metaphor; D is contradicted by the whole passage.
6. **D** — ¶E: *"a lawyer's hour"* is valued above *"a cleaner's"*. A is too absolute; B is false because the method uses paid earnings; C is not stated.
7. **A** — ¶E: a wage can make care *"look like a service that can be cut"*. B is not claimed; C is the opposite of the passage; D is not discussed.
8. **C** — ¶F: *"refusing to measure care is not a neutral act either"*. A is not the writer's view (the passage accepts satellite accounts); B is explicitly denied; D is an overreaction not proposed.
9. **YES** — ¶B: *"includes the rent that owner-occupiers would pay ... impute a value"*.
10. **NO** — ¶C: respondents record *"primary activities ... alongside secondary ones"*.
11. **NOT GIVEN** — ¶F reports that *"some advocates conclude"* carers should be paid, but the writer does not endorse it; both positions are called *"defensible"*.
12. **YES** — ¶E: satellite accounts *"sit alongside GDP rather than replacing it"*.

### Strategy — Dense text & inference

1. **Đọc câu chủ đề của mỗi đoạn trước.** Với ¶A, câu cuối thường nêu luận điểm; với ¶B–E, câu đầu là chủ đề, câu cuối là hệ quả.
2. **Tách câu dài thành ba tầng:** mệnh đề chính (ai làm gì), mệnh đề phụ (điều kiện/nhượng bộ), cụm danh từ trừu tượng. Đừng dịch từng từ.
3. **Inference = evidence + một bước.** Ví dụ: *"satellite accounts sit alongside GDP"* → chúng bổ sung, không thay thế. Hai bước (*"vì vậy chính phủ sẽ cải cách"*) là suy diễn quá xa.
4. **Dự đoán trước khi đọc options.** Nếu bạn đã nghĩ "thiếu trong thống kê", option B ở câu 1 sẽ nổi bật; các option khác khó dụ hơn.
5. **Với Y/N/NG:** YES = cùng nghĩa; NO = trái trực tiếp; NG = không có căn cứ, kể cả khi ngoài đời điều đó đúng.
6. **Đánh dấu phạm vi:** *some, most, always, may, in part*. Một từ như *always* thường biến câu đúng thành câu sai.

### Common mistakes

- Chọn đáp án **đúng nhưng không trả lời câu hỏi** (ví dụ câu 1 chọn A vì bài nói công việc mệt, nhưng câu hỏi hỏi *mục đích của ví dụ*).
- Biến *"can make care look cuttable"* thành *"will cause cuts"* — mất mức độ chắc chắn.
- Với NG, dùng kiến thức ngoài bài để khẳng định điều passage không nói.
- Bỏ qua câu cuối đoạn, nơi tác giả thường đặt hàm ý.

## Unit 2 — Writer's Purpose, Attitude & Tone

**Mục tiêu:** Nhận diện mục đích của đoạn/từng câu, thái độ và giọng điệu; phân biệt *critical, sceptical, nostalgic, balanced*.  
**Độ dài:** ~940 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 11 · **Thời gian:** 18 phút.

### Reading passage

**The Amateur's Advantage**

**A.** On a wet morning in a wood in southern England, a retired teacher photographs a moth on a gatepost. She uploads the image to a national recording scheme; an identifier confirms that it is a species rarely seen in that county. The wood has not been surveyed by a professional entomologist for fifteen years. In the record book, the sighting becomes a single dot. What makes it interesting is not the moth but the assumption it unsettles: that reliable knowledge is produced by people who are paid to produce it. The teacher has no laboratory, no grant and no formal qualification in ecology. She has something else — twenty years of walking the same paths, and the habit of noticing when something is out of place. Her notebook is the only continuous record of the site, and continuity is exactly what a short-funded survey cannot buy.

**B.** The division between amateur and professional is recent. In the nineteenth century, science moved into universities, laboratories and salaried careers. Before that, most naturalists earned their livings elsewhere: Darwin on the Beagle was a gentleman companion, Mendel a monk, Wallace a surveyor. Professionalisation brought standards, instruments and regular income, and it also brought a new vocabulary. *Amateur* shifted from a description of someone who loves a subject to a description of someone who cannot be trusted with it. The word has become an insult disguised as a description. Yet the field sciences never escaped their dependence on unpaid observers. Weather, birds, insects, archaeological sites and the night sky are all monitored by people whose work is described, in grant applications, as "citizen data". The category is broad enough to include a birdwatcher with a notebook and a farmer who reports the first swallow of spring.

**C.** The strengths of amateur observation are specific. Local knowledge is one: a volunteer who walks a site every week knows where water collects and when a species first appears. Continuity is another. A professional may visit a reserve four times a year; a volunteer may have recorded the same hedge for two decades, producing the kind of long series that no short project can buy. Amateurs are also good at anomalies, because they see the ordinary often enough to notice when it changes. The weaknesses are equally specific: inconsistent methods, misidentification, and effort bias — the tendency to record rare species more diligently than common ones. Good citizen-science schemes respond with training, specimen vouchers, moderation and statistical models that estimate what observers miss. Validation improves the reliability of the data. It does not, by itself, answer the harder question of whose questions count. The best schemes treat this as a design problem rather than a public-relations one, publishing their methods and their error rates. The result is a form of quality control that is social as well as statistical, and it depends on volunteers understanding why the rules exist.

**D.** The dependence is real; the acknowledgement is selective. Phenology — the timing of leafing, flowering and migration — now provides some of the clearest evidence of climate change, and much of that evidence comes from diaries kept by volunteers. Scientists cite the records while describing their authors as "data collectors", a phrase that assigns labour but not insight. This is not merely a matter of manners. It shapes who is invited to interpret findings, who appears on papers and who is funded to continue. The person who first noticed that the swifts were arriving earlier is rarely the person who explains why it matters. That asymmetry is not unique to ecology. Amateurs are treated as sensors: valued for what they detect, discounted for what they might conclude. Credit is not a courtesy; it is a resource that shapes who can continue the work.

**E.** There is a political economy beneath this. Monitoring budgets have tightened, and volunteers have filled the gap. In some countries, the majority of wildlife records now come from unpaid observers. That arrangement can be mutually beneficial, but it can also be extractive. Volunteers pay for their own travel and equipment, receive little training and are rarely credited in publications. "Volunteer enthusiasm is not a renewable resource," one coordinator observes; burnout and turnover are constant risks. The sustainable version of citizen science pays for coordination, shares data with the people who collect it and treats volunteers as partners rather than as free labour. Enthusiasm can be spent. It cannot be stockpiled. A programme that relies on goodwill without funding is not a partnership; it is a subsidy with a thank-you note. The alternative is not to pay every volunteer, but to stop pretending that coordination is free.

**F.** None of this justifies romanticising the amateur. Untrained observers misidentify species, miss what they are not looking for and sometimes see patterns that are artefacts of where they like to walk. Standards exist for good reasons, and expertise is not a fiction. But professionalism is not the only route to rigour, and the hierarchy that places the salaried expert at the top and the volunteer at the bottom obscures how much each needs the other. The amateur is not a cheaper professional; the amateur is a different kind of witness. The most productive arrangements combine the two: professionals frame questions and design protocols, amateurs supply continuity and local presence, and both take part in interpretation. The aim is not to collapse the hierarchy but to make it honest.

### Reading passage

**The Seed Vault Paradox**

**A.** The entrance is a narrow concrete wedge emerging from a mountainside in Svalbard, above the Arctic Circle. Behind it, a tunnel leads into permafrost and three sealed chambers. The popular image of the facility is one of insurance: a doomsday vault that will preserve the world's crop diversity whatever happens outside. The reality is more prosaic. The vault is a backup for seed collections held elsewhere, a place where duplicate samples can wait in cold storage while the institutions that own them continue their ordinary work. It is engineered to survive earthquakes, power failures and, at least in principle, the collapse of the societies that built it. It is not designed to be opened every day. The site was chosen for its cold, its remoteness and its distance from the sea, not because Svalbard has any special claim on the world's crops. The three chambers are kept at different temperatures, and the outermost is used for visits rather than storage. The permafrost provides a natural freezer, but the technology is mundane.

**B.** The case for such a backup begins with uniformity. Twentieth-century agriculture spread a small number of high-yielding varieties across enormous areas, displacing landraces that farmers had selected over generations for local soils, tastes and pests. The gains were real: famine receded in much of Asia, and food became cheaper. But genetic uniformity is efficient until it is not. A crop that is identical everywhere is also vulnerable everywhere, and the Irish potato famine remains the standard warning about what happens when a single variety meets a single pathogen. The lesson is not that farmers were foolish; it is that efficiency and exposure can rise together. The varieties that disappeared were not necessarily inferior; they were merely less profitable to sell. Diversity is not a sentimental attachment to old seeds. It is a portfolio of responses to conditions that no one can fully predict. The logic is that of an insurance portfolio: many small bets, held precisely because most of them will never be needed.

**C.** Stored seeds are not frozen in a state of permanent readiness. They age. Viability declines, slowly in the vault's −18°C chambers but not infinitely, and samples must periodically be withdrawn, sown and regenerated. That cycle requires land, glasshouses, skilled staff and money, and it can take years for a slow-growing tree or a biennial root crop. A collection that is never regenerated gradually becomes a collection of dead packets. Curators sometimes describe the vault as a library, but it is a library that must be read to survive: the books decay unless someone opens them, checks them and makes new copies. Some seeds survive for decades; others, particularly those of tropical trees, lose viability within a few years and demand frequent attention. Regeneration is therefore not a rare emergency measure but a routine cost of doing conservation properly. That cost is rarely counted in the price of a seed.

**D.** The vault also sits inside a legal landscape. Depositors retain ownership of their samples, and access is governed by a patchwork of national laws, institutional agreements and international treaties. The arrangement is often described as a form of global cooperation, and it is, but the cooperation is bounded. A country may deposit seeds and still refuse to release them freely; a genebank may hold material that breeders in another country cannot legally obtain. The vault is not a supermarket of genes. It is a storage facility whose contents remain subject to the politics of the countries that sent them. The result is a facility that is global in its symbolism and national in its jurisdiction. Even the treaty that governs plant genetic resources cannot override the interests of the states that signed it.

**E.** Even a viable sample is only useful if someone knows what it contains. A seed's value lies in traits that must be observed, measured and recorded: resistance to a disease, tolerance of salt, a particular response to heat. This work of characterisation is slow and underfunded, and much of it is still incomplete. Breeders also need access — not merely legal permission, but the practical ability to find a sample, request it and receive it in time for a breeding cycle. Frozen diversity is potential, not capacity. The distinction matters because it is easy to confuse the size of a collection with the security it provides. A vault full of uncharacterised seeds is a promise whose value cannot yet be assessed. Characterisation is therefore infrastructure, not a preliminary.

**F.** The deepest paradox is political. The vault has become a symbol of planetary foresight, a photograph that ministers can stand in front of. That symbolism is not worthless; it attracts attention and money. But it can also distract from the less photogenic work that keeps crop diversity alive: farmers saving seed, genebanks regenerating accessions, breeders crossing plants in glasshouses, technicians checking germination rates. Concrete is not a substitute for cultivation. Conservation is a practice rather than a single act of storage, and it depends on coordination among the people who store, study and grow plants. The vault protects a backup. It cannot protect the system that makes the backup meaningful. The image of the vault survives because it is easier to photograph than a rotation of crops. The harder work is invisible in the same way that care work is invisible: it happens in fields, not in photographs.

**The Attention Economy Is Not a Metaphor**

**A.** Attention is not a new scarcity. Every generation has complained that something was competing for its focus, whether the novel, the radio or the television. What is new is the scale and precision of the competition. Digital platforms can measure how long a glance lasts and adjust what appears next within milliseconds. The result is a market in which attention is both the resource being sought and the price being paid. The phrase "attention economy" is often used as a metaphor. It is not. Attention is limited relative to demand, it can be allocated, and it can be sold. What makes the market unusual is that the people whose attention is traded rarely experience a transaction. The consequence is that the market's costs are felt as personal failure rather than as a price. The word "economy" does real work here: it makes a relationship look like a market. The market is not natural; it is built.

**B.** Platforms do not sell attention itself; they sell access to it, priced by the probability of a glance. Advertisers bid for a slot, and the platform's algorithm decides which slot is most likely to produce the desired response. The product being traded is therefore a prediction: a forecast that a particular person, at a particular moment, will pause. This is why engagement metrics matter so much. Time spent, clicks and shares are not neutral measurements of success; they are the raw material from which predictions are made. A platform that keeps users scrolling is not necessarily serving them better. It is producing a more valuable forecast. Because the forecast improves with more data, the system has an interest in learning more about the person being predicted. The economics of the model reward sharper prediction, not greater satisfaction. The user sees content; the advertiser buys a probability.

**C.** The cognitive costs of this arrangement are real, but they are easy to describe badly. Laboratory studies show that switching between tasks slows people down and increases errors; surveys find that notifications fragment working memory and make sustained reading harder. Field evidence is more mixed. Some workers report that constant connectivity helps them respond to clients; some students perform better with controlled use than with abstinence. Effect sizes are often modest, and the panic in public discussion exceeds the evidence. None of this makes the costs trivial. It means that harm is unevenly distributed: people who already struggle with attention, or whose jobs demand constant responsiveness, are more exposed than the average user. The distinction between a modest average effect and a serious individual harm is central to the debate, and it is usually lost in headlines. The average, in other words, hides the tail.

**D.** Comparing attention to pollution is attractive because it captures something true: both are harms produced by otherwise beneficial activity. It also clarifies why individual solutions are insufficient. A person can choose to use a platform less, just as a person can choose to drive less, but neither choice changes the structure that makes the harmful option convenient. Yet the pollution analogy has a limit. Pollution is usually a by-product of an activity whose main purpose lies elsewhere; capturing attention is not a by-product of the platform business, it is the business. This distinction matters for regulation. If attention capture is the product, then asking a company to reduce it is asking it to damage its own revenue model, not merely to clean up after itself. That is a much harder political request than a demand for cleaner technology. Analogies are useful precisely until they become arguments.

**E.** The problem is structural, and structural problems are hard to solve one person at a time. Design choices that make notifications difficult to disable, or that reward outrage, are not accidents; they are arrangements that profit from impulsiveness. Digital literacy programmes ask individuals to resist such arrangements, and they can help at the margins, but they place the burden on the person with the least information and the least power. Regulation is a collective action problem: no single user can change the default, and no single company can afford to change it alone if competitors do not. This is why policy debates keep returning to defaults, interoperability and limits on data collection rather than to willpower. The question is not whether people should be more disciplined, but why the environment is designed to make discipline so costly. Defaults are political because they decide who must act.

**F.** The metaphor of theft, which is implicit in much of the criticism, encourages a mistaken view: that attention is a possession taken from us. But attention is not stolen like a wallet. It is cultivated — by habit, by environment, by the expectations of the people around us. A child who grows up in a household where adults look up when spoken to learns a different relationship to focus from one who does not. The question, then, is not how to protect a fixed stock of attention but how to govern the conditions under which attention is grown. That is a slower and less satisfying question than "who stole my focus?", but it is the one that connects the psychology of individuals to the design of institutions. It also explains why moralising about screen time so rarely changes anything. That is a less satisfying conclusion, and a more useful one.

### Strategy — Vocabulary & complex sentences

1. **Đừng dịch từ đơn.** Đọc câu trước và câu sau; từ C1 thường được định nghĩa ngay trong đoạn bằng dấu phẩy, dấu hai chấm hoặc *that is*.

3. **Tách câu phức:** khoanh mệnh đề chính, gạch *although, while, because, only when, not ... but*. Dịch mệnh đề chính trước.

5. **Với *only when*,** điều kiện là cần thiết, không phải lúc nào cũng đủ. Đừng biến thành *whenever*.
6. **Câu hỏi reference:** tìm danh từ/cụm danh từ gần nhất phù hợp về số ít/nhiều và logic, không chỉ vị trí gần nhất.

### Reading passage

**Night Trains and the Arithmetic of Revival**

**A.** For much of the 2000s, the sleeper train appeared to be disappearing. Routes that had once carried diplomats, students and families across Europe were cancelled one by one as operators concluded that cheap flights and high-speed day trains had made them obsolete. Carriages were sold, depots closed, and the knowledge required to maintain them thinned out. Then the direction reversed. An Austrian operator expanded its night network, France and Sweden announced new services, and several smaller companies began running seasonal routes. The revival is real, but it is uneven. Many new services run only a few times a week, use ageing rolling stock and depend on public subsidies to cover their costs. The revival is therefore less a market triumph than a political decision to keep a service alive. The carriages themselves became a niche skill, preserved by a handful of depots. The result is still a patchwork of routes rather than a network.

**B.** The economics are awkward. A sleeper carries fewer passengers than a seated train because beds take up space, and the carriages are expensive to build and maintain. Revenue is earned only when the train is moving, so a night service makes one long trip and then waits while a short-haul aircraft might complete several rotations in the same period. Track access charges, shunting and staff costs continue whether the train is full or half empty. Aviation enjoys an advantage that is rarely mentioned in debates about passenger choice: in most countries, fuel for international flights is not taxed. The comparison, therefore, is not between two modes competing on equal terms. It is between a transport system that pays for its infrastructure and one that does not. That asymmetry is the single most important fact in the economics of night travel. A plane can be reused; a night train arrives and waits.

**C.** The environmental case is strong but conditional. Per passenger-kilometre, rail generally emits less than short-haul aviation, especially when trains are full and the electricity supply is low-carbon. A half-empty sleeper, or one powered by coal-fired electricity, narrows the gap sharply. The useful question is not whether trains are greener in general but whether a particular route, at a particular occupancy, displaces a particular flight. Capacity is the binding constraint. A train can attract drivers only if there are berths, and it can attract them only if the timetable connects with the rest of the network. Green intentions do not board trains; seats and connections do. Yet the green case is often presented as if it were unconditional, which makes the eventual disappointment easier to exploit. A single half-empty train can be photographed as proof that the whole idea is fantasy. The same arithmetic applies to buses and coaches.

**D.** For passengers, the appeal is different. A night train turns travel time into sleep and delivers people into the centre of a city at breakfast. Some travellers describe the experience as a hotel that moves; others describe it as a cramped hostel with strangers and a shared bathroom. Privacy, noise, temperature and the quality of the coffee all matter, and they matter more when the journey lasts ten hours. The experience divides travellers, and the division is not purely about price. A business traveller with an early meeting may value a flat bed; a family may value the absence of an airport; a student may value the fare. The same train can serve all three badly or one of them well. Operators know this, which is why the same route may be marketed as an adventure to some travellers and as a business service to others. Comfort is not a luxury in a market where the alternative is a flight.

**E.** The politics are harder than the engineering. Cross-border services require coordination on safety rules, signalling systems, staffing and ticket sales. Each country has its own procedures, and a carriage approved in one may need modification before it can run in another. Rolling stock must be compatible with several power systems. Subsidies are national, while the benefits — fewer flights, lower emissions, connections between regions — are international. A route can be rational for Europe as a whole and irrational for any single finance ministry. The result is a familiar pattern: everyone agrees in principle, and no one wants to pay for the part of the journey that crosses the border. This is not a failure of goodwill so much as a mismatch between the scale of the problem and the scale of the budget. No single ministry owns the whole journey.

**F.** None of this means that night trains will replace short-haul flights. They are a niche, and a fragile one, dependent on subsidies, old carriages and a limited pool of trained staff. But niches can matter out of proportion to their size. The night train is a test of whether Europe can plan beyond a single budget cycle, and whether governments can value a service whose benefits arrive slowly and are shared across borders. Its future depends less on nostalgia than on procurement, track charges, and a willingness to pay for something that is easy to romanticise and harder to run. The romance is real. The arithmetic is real too. What is missing is a political structure capable of holding both in view at once. That is a policy problem, not a romance problem.

### Reading passage

**The Memory of Rivers**

**A.** A river that has been straightened still carries the memory of its old course. In fields beside the channel, former meanders show up as darker bands of soil; after heavy rain, water gathers in hollows where the river once turned. *A river remembers* is a metaphor, but a precise one. The landscape retains the geometry of past flow, and water still follows that geometry when the engineered channel cannot cope. Nineteenth-century engineers straightened rivers to speed drainage and protect land from flooding. They succeeded often enough to be copied across Europe and North America, and the consequences — faster currents, deeper channels, fewer wetlands — took decades to become visible. The river did not forget its old shape. It was given a new one and continued to respond to both. The old channel is not a ghost; it is a physical fact that the new channel has to negotiate with every time it floods.

**B.** Restoration has changed its ambitions. Early projects often tried to recreate a picturesque image of a river: a sinuous channel, grassy banks, a few stepping stones. Contemporary schemes are more likely to aim at processes — sediment moving downstream, water spreading onto the floodplain in winter, dead wood lodging in bends and creating pools. The difference matters. A restored channel that looks right but cannot move sediment will silt up or scour unexpectedly; a river allowed to shift may look messy and still be healthier. A river that is allowed to move is harder to certify. "We are not building a museum exhibit," one ecologist says. "We are trying to give the river the conditions to make its own decisions." That means removing weirs, reconnecting side channels, planting riparian trees and, sometimes, doing nothing at all.

**C.** The science is less certain than the advocacy sometimes suggests. Adding wood to a river can create pools that shelter young fish, trap sediment and diversify flow, but it can also raise water levels and increase flood risk for a nearby road or house. Reconnecting a floodplain can reduce flood peaks downstream, but the effect depends on the size of the flood, the shape of the valley and the order in which interventions are carried out. Monitoring is typically short — three to five years, often tied to a funding cycle — while ecological change may take decades. Restoration is therefore an experiment conducted in public, with uncertain results and a constituency that expects visible improvement. The honest position is not that restoration fails, but that its outcomes are conditional and slow. The most useful studies are often those that report failure, because they show which conditions matter.

**D.** Beavers make the argument concrete. Hunted to extinction in Britain by the early modern period, they have been reintroduced in several catchments, sometimes officially and sometimes not. Their dams slow water, raise water tables, trap sediment and create wetlands that support insects, amphibians and birds. They also flood farmland, block culverts and alter the appearance of a valley in ways that neighbours may not welcome. Compensation schemes exist, but they are uneven and slow. Some farmers have become enthusiastic partners; others regard the animal as a threat to their livelihood. The conflict is not really about ecology. It is about who bears the cost of a public benefit, and whether the rules that govern that cost are seen as fair. As one participant put it, the animal is not the problem; the rules are.

**E.** Floodplains expose a similar distributional question. Giving a river room to flood can lower peaks downstream, recharge groundwater and create habitat, but the land used for flooding has to come from somewhere. If it is bought from willing sellers, the public pays twice: once for the land and once for the flood defence that is no longer needed. If it is taken from farmers, the costs fall on a few while the benefits are spread across a city. And the people who live on floodplains are rarely the people with the most political influence. Space for water is not a neutral technical category. It is a political allocation of risk, and it tends to reproduce existing inequalities unless someone deliberately intervenes. That is why the most successful schemes often begin with a conversation about compensation rather than a map of the floodplain.

**F.** Restoration also has to decide what "natural" means. The baseline is usually taken to be some period before industrial engineering, but that period was itself shaped by centuries of farming, drainage and fishing. A river restored to its 1850 condition may be less resilient than one allowed to change; a floodplain managed for a particular species may be less diverse than one left alone. Ecologists call this the shifting baseline problem: each generation assumes that the landscape it grew up with is the normal one, and measures change against that memory. The alternative is not to abandon targets but to choose them for resilience rather than resemblance. The aim should be a river that can absorb disturbance, not a replica of a particular past.

**G.** None of this can be delivered by a single project or a single agency. Catchments cross municipal and national boundaries; the effects of an intervention upstream are felt downstream, sometimes years later. That mismatch between ecological and political time is the central obstacle, and it is not one that any single project can solve. Funding cycles are short, political attention shorter, and monitoring is often the first item to be cut. Yet restoration succeeds where institutions manage to coordinate across those boundaries and where local people have a genuine role in deciding what is being restored and for whom. That is less satisfying than the image of a river returning to its old self. It is also more honest. Restoration is not a return. It is a practice of negotiation, conducted in a landscape that is already a palimpsest of human decisions. The work is slow because trust is slow. It is also necessary because water does not respect boundaries.

11. **E** — ¶E: costs fall on a few, benefits spread; floodplain residents lack political influence.
12. **G** — ¶G: *"Funding cycles are short ... and monitoring is often the first item to be cut"*, while effects are felt *"sometimes years later"*.
13. **fixed state** — ¶F: *"The aim is not to recreate a fixed state but to restore the processes"* (also ¶B). *Past state* is less precise and is not the phrase used; the gap requires the contrast with processes.
14. **resilience** — ¶F: *"The aim should be a river that can absorb disturbance"* and *"choose them for resilience rather than resemblance"*.

## C1 Exit Checklist

Bạn sẵn sàng thi Academic Reading ở mục tiêu 7.5–8.0 khi:

- [ ] Hoàn thành một passage 900–1.100 từ trong **17–18 phút**, kể cả kiểm tra đáp án.
- [ ] Đạt **11/13** hoặc **12/14** ở hai bài mock liên tiếp, không dùng từ điển.
- [ ] Với mỗi đáp án đúng, chỉ ra được **đoạn và câu** làm bằng chứng trong vòng 10 giây.
- [ ] Giải thích được vì sao **ba đáp án sai** sai, không chỉ vì sao đáp án đúng đúng.
- [ ] Phân biệt thành thạo **stated / implied / not given**; không suy diễn hai bước.
- [ ] Nhận diện được **purpose, attitude, tone** và không nhầm *critical* với *hostile*, *qualified* với *wholly*.
- [ ] Làm đúng **Matching Information** mà không dựa vào từ khóa nguyên văn.
- [ ] Hoàn thành **Summary Completion** với từ trừu tượng, kiểm tra ngữ pháp của gap.
- [ ] Giải mã câu có *not X but Y*, *only when*, phủ định kép và mệnh đề quan hệ lồng nhau.
- [ ] Giữ **tỷ lệ lỗi do bất cẩn (C) dưới 1 câu/passage** và không bỏ trống câu nào.
- [ ] Áp dụng được **45-second rule** khi matching: không mắc kẹt quá lâu ở một câu.
- [ ] Trong full test 3 passage, hoàn thành passage 1 trong 16–17 phút và còn 3–4 phút kiểm tra.
- [ ] Có sổ lỗi với ít nhất **30 câu sai** đã được phân tích theo mã V/R/L/P/Q/T/C.
- [ ] Đạt **33–36/40** trong một full test tự làm, trong điều kiện không gián đoạn.
