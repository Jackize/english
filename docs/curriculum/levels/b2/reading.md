## Unit 1 — Inference

**Mục tiêu:** Đọc điều người viết ngụ ý; phân biệt *stated* và *implied*.  
**Độ dài:** ~740 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 10 · **Thời gian:** 20 phút.

### Reading passage

**Rewilding the British Uplands**

**A.** The uplands of Britain are often described as wild, yet they are among the most heavily managed landscapes in Europe. For perhaps eight thousand years, farming communities have cleared woodland, grazed livestock and burned heather. What visitors see today — mile upon mile of close-cropped grass and dark heather — is not the product of nature left alone but of nature held in place. The sheep that maintain this appearance are themselves a creation of policy: for much of the twentieth century, subsidies rewarded farmers for the number of animals they kept, regardless of whether the land could comfortably support them. Even the heather was burned to order, in strips, so that young shoots would feed grouse.

**B.** By the 1990s, a small group of ecologists had begun to argue that the apparent permanence of these hills was an illusion. Heather moorland supports relatively few species, they noted, and the burning that keeps it open also strips the soil of carbon that has taken centuries to accumulate. Worse, the drainage channels cut to dry the peat bogs — many dug by hand during periods of rural unemployment — continued to release carbon long after the workers who dug them had gone. The argument was not that farmers were villains. It was that a system designed to produce sheep and grouse had quietly become a system for producing carbon dioxide.

**C.** The alternative they proposed was rewilding: the restoration of natural processes rather than the maintenance of a particular view. In practice this usually means reducing grazing pressure, blocking drainage channels and, occasionally, reintroducing species that vanished centuries ago. The results can be startling. On a stretch of the Cambrian Mountains, a project that began by simply removing sheep recorded a doubling of tree cover within fifteen years, much of it grown from seeds that had lain dormant in the soil. Beavers, released experimentally in Devon, built dams that held back water during storms and created ponds in which amphibians and insects flourished.

**D.** Yet rewilding has proved far more contentious than its scientific case might suggest. Opponents point out that the landscapes people value — the open moorland painted by artists, the nesting grounds of rare upland birds such as the curlew — depend on the very grazing that rewilding would remove. Others warn of the economic consequences for communities whose incomes rest on sheep farming and grouse shooting. These are not trivial objections, and the debate has sometimes been conducted as though one side must lose. But the choice is rarely between a working landscape and an empty one; it is between different kinds of work, different kinds of beauty, and different groups of people who benefit.

**E.** Economists have begun to put a price on the services that restored ecosystems provide: flood protection, carbon storage, water purification, even the quiet that visitors travel to find. In principle, payments for these services could replace the subsidies that currently reward degradation. In practice, designing such schemes is difficult. The benefits accrue slowly and to everyone; the costs fall immediately on a few. A farmer who stops grazing a hillside may wait twenty years for woodland to mature, and cannot easily sell that future to a bank.

**F.** Perhaps the deepest obstacle is cultural. The British have long imagined their uplands as empty, and emptiness is easier to love than complexity. A hillside grazed by sheep looks cared for; a hillside returning to scrub can look neglected, even to people who would approve of it in principle. Conservation bodies have learned that the same photograph can be read as either restoration or abandonment, depending on what the viewer expects to see. Rewilding, in other words, is as much a project of imagination as of ecology. It asks people to accept that a landscape they have been taught to admire was in part a factory, and that its replacement may be less picturesque before it becomes more alive.

**G.** None of this means the case for a wilder countryside is weak. It means that the case has to be made politically as well as scientifically, with money attached and with a role for the people who live there. Where projects have succeeded, they have usually done so because local residents were given employment in them — fencing, monitoring, guiding — rather than merely consulted at the end. The uplands will not become wild again by being abandoned. They may become wilder by being worked differently.

### Strategy — Inference

1. Inference = **evidence + một bước suy luận**; cần hai bước là sai.
2. Đọc câu **trước và sau** keyword; hàm ý nằm ở *but, yet, in other words*.
3. Chú ý từ đánh giá: *quietly, startling, perhaps the deepest obstacle*.
4. Loại đáp án dựa trên kiến thức ngoài bài hoặc quá cực đoan.
5. Short Answer: copy nguyên cụm, không chia lại động từ, không thêm mạo từ.

### Common mistakes

- Chọn đáp án **được nói rõ** thay vì **được ngụ ý**.
- Suy diễn xa: từ "beavers build dams" kết luận "beavers sẽ được thả khắp Anh".
- Bỏ qua từ giới hạn (*mainly, in part*) hoặc viết 4 từ khi đề cho 3.

## Unit 2 — Writer's Attitude & Tone

**Mục tiêu:** Nhận diện quan điểm và mức độ ủng hộ/phản đối qua từ ngữ.  
**Độ dài:** ~720 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 10 MCQ · **Thời gian:** 20 phút.

### Reading passage

**The Four-Day Week and the Trouble with Good News**

**A.** When the results of a six-month trial of the four-day week were published in 2022, the headlines wrote themselves. Sixty-one British companies had taken part; most reported that revenue had held steady, resignations had fallen and staff felt less stressed. Within days the four-day week had been declared a success, a failure, a luxury for the middle classes and the future of work — sometimes in the same newspaper. The trial was organised by an advocacy group, a detail that appeared in few of the reports, and the figures were repeated without the caveats that had accompanied them in the original document. Readers could be forgiven for concluding that the experiment had settled a question it had barely begun to ask. The coverage was a preview of how work reforms are now discussed: as personal liberation or as economic threat, rarely as an accounting problem.

**B.** The design of the trial deserves closer attention than it received. The firms that took part were volunteers, and volunteers for a shorter working week are unlikely to be typical: they tend to be small, service-based and already persuaded that the change would work. Firms that expected to struggle did not put themselves forward, which makes the sample even less representative. There was no control group, so any improvement might have come from the attention the firms received rather than from the extra day off. Productivity, moreover, is notoriously hard to measure in the sectors involved. When a marketing agency reports that output stayed the same, it usually means that managers judged it to have stayed the same. None of this is fraud. Trials of this kind are pilots, and pilots are designed to be promising.

**C.** The findings are not worthless for being partial. The trial demonstrated something narrower but still important: that a substantial number of organisations can reorganise their work so that meetings shrink, email declines and the same tasks are completed in less time. Those gains deserve to be taken seriously, even by readers who doubt the productivity figures. Many employees described the fourth day as the first time in years they had been able to attend to their own lives — medical appointments, elderly parents, children. Even if the productivity gains were partly an effect of being watched, the human gains were real to the people who reported them.

**D.** The more interesting question is not whether firms can compress five days into four, but which firms cannot. A hospital cannot close a ward on Fridays; a restaurant cannot serve four days of customers in three; a care home cannot shorten the hours in which residents need help with eating and washing. The trial spoke for a segment of the economy — professional services, technology, marketing — where output is difficult to measure and therefore easy to reorganise. It said almost nothing about the sectors where hours are fixed by the presence of other human beings, and those are also the sectors where staff shortages are most acute. Policy designed in a government office rarely survives contact with a shift rota.

**E.** There is a question of fairness too. For a software company, paying the same salary for four days is a modest cost, absorbed by lower office overheads and reduced turnover. For a care provider on thin margins, it is a straightforward increase in the hourly cost of labour. A benefit that reaches only the comfortably employed is not a reform; it is a perk. If the four-day week spreads only where work is already pleasant, it may widen the gap it claims to close. A reform that depends on the goodwill of the office will not reach the people who most need it.

**F.** I find myself persuaded that the four-day week is a good idea whose time has come unevenly. It should be encouraged, tested and, in some workplaces, negotiated; it should not be legislated into existence everywhere at once. The strongest argument for it is not the one made in the headlines — that it costs nothing — but the older argument that time is a good in itself, and that a society which cannot imagine distributing it more evenly has stopped asking serious questions about what work is for. That is a claim about values, and no trial, however well designed, can test it.

### Strategy — Attitude & Tone

1. Tìm **stance sentence**, thường ở đoạn cuối hoặc sau *but / yet*.
2. Chú ý **mức độ**: *persuaded, doubtful* mạnh hơn *partly, slightly*.
3. Hỏi **thái độ về cái gì**: bài có thể chỉ trích báo chí, hoãn luật, nhưng ủng hộ ý tưởng.
4. Loại đáp án cực đoan khi bài có hedging (*furious, contemptuous*).
5. Giọng điệu ≠ nội dung: câu tiêu cực vẫn có thể viết bằng giọng hài hước.

- Chọn *neutral* khi người viết dùng nhiều từ đánh giá; bỏ qua irony (*"wrote themselves"*).

## Unit 3 — Yes/No/Not Given

**Mục tiêu:** Phân biệt ý kiến người viết với sự kiện; xử lý NO và NOT GIVEN.  
**Độ dài:** ~710 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 10 Y/N/NG · **Thời gian:** 20 phút.

### Reading passage

**What Tests Can and Cannot Tell Us**

**A.** Few topics in education attract as much confident opinion as testing, and few are supported by such uneven evidence. Public argument arranges itself into two camps. In one, examinations guarantee standards: they are impersonal, comparable and resistant to favouritism. In the other, tests manufacture anxiety, narrow what is taught and reward a particular kind of memory. Both camps overstate what research can tell us, largely because they are answering a question — are tests good? — that cannot be answered in general. A test is a tool, and tools are good or bad for purposes. Each camp has its studies; neither has the whole picture.

**B.** Cognitive psychology provides the strongest evidence in favour of testing, though not in the form its defenders usually cite. Experiments on retrieval practice show that students required to recall material — in a quiz, a short written answer, even a flashcard — remember more a week later than students who spend the same time rereading. The effect is robust across ages and subjects, and it is one of the few findings in education that has been replicated often enough to be trusted. The explanation is that retrieving information strengthens the route back to it, while rereading creates a feeling of familiarity that is easily mistaken for knowledge. But the effect is strongest for the recall of specific facts, and weaker or absent when students must apply ideas to unfamiliar problems. A test can strengthen memory and still fail to measure understanding.

**C.** Evidence on accountability is more troubling. When schools are judged by scores in a narrow set of subjects, they respond rationally: they give more time to those subjects and less to everything else. Studies of the American No Child Left Behind reforms found modest gains in the tested subjects, particularly among the lowest achievers, alongside reductions in science, history and the arts — reductions that were largest in schools serving poorer communities. Teachers in affected schools described spending weeks on test technique, a skill that transfers poorly to anything else. The schools that gained most were often those with the strongest leadership, which is another way of saying that the reform rewarded advantage. Whether the trade was worth making is a question of values rather than measurement, and no dataset can settle it.

**D.** Classroom assessment raises different questions. Researchers distinguish between assessment **of** learning, which summarises what a student has achieved, and assessment **for** learning, which is meant to shape the next lesson. The second is more valuable and much harder. Feedback that consists of a mark teaches little; feedback that describes what to do next can be powerful, but it takes time that teachers rarely have, and marking of this kind is the first thing to disappear when a timetable is full. England's removal of national curriculum levels in 2014 was intended to reduce the false precision that levels encouraged, and several schools replaced them with numbered bands that were levels by another name.

**E.** Public confidence depends on comparability, and comparability is fragile. When results rise year after year, the public suspects that examinations have become easier, even when the rise reflects better teaching. That suspicion is not always unreasonable. During the pandemic, the shift to teacher-assessed grades produced record results and a corresponding loss of confidence; universities had to decide how much to trust a grade awarded by people who knew the candidate. The gap between what a grade means and what it measures has rarely been so visible. International comparisons such as PISA are read as league tables although their own designers warn against this: differences in sampling, translation and curricula make small gaps meaningless. Trust, once lost in a grading system, is slow to return.

**F.** None of this argues for abolishing examinations, which remain the least bad method of comparing large numbers of students fairly. It argues for designing assessment around its purpose, using more than one instrument, and publishing the uncertainty rather than hiding it. It also means telling students what each assessment is for, since a test whose purpose is unclear invites both anxiety and contempt. A system that admits what it cannot measure is more trustworthy than one that claims to measure everything. That is a modest ambition, and an unusually difficult one.

### Vocabulary

- **retrieval (n)** — sự truy hồi trí nhớ — *retrieval practice*
- **robust (adj)** — vững, đáng tin — *the effect is robust*
- **replicate (v)** — lặp lại nghiên cứu — *replicated often enough*

- **comparability (n)** — tính so sánh được — *depends on comparability*
- **league table** — bảng xếp hạng — *read as league tables*
- **sampling (n)** — việc lấy mẫu — *differences in sampling*
- **uncertainty (n)** — sự không chắc chắn — *publishing the uncertainty*

### Strategy — Yes/No/Not Given

1. Đây là câu hỏi về **ý kiến**, không phải sự kiện: *người viết có tin điều này không?*
2. Tìm **stance sentence** qua *should, cannot, troubling, overstate, the least bad*.
3. **NO** khi bài nói ngược hoặc phủ định; **NOT GIVEN** khi bài im lặng.
4. Cẩn thận câu hỏi kép: *"equally strong"*, *"justified"*.
5. Đừng suy đoán điều người viết "chắc hẳn" nghĩ.

### Common mistakes

- Dùng logic T/F/NG: tìm sự kiện thay vì quan điểm.
- Thấy bài nêu *"gains alongside reductions"* rồi kết luận người viết ủng hộ cắt giảm.
- Chọn **NO** cho câu 6 vì đoán giáo viên không thích; đúng phải là **NOT GIVEN**.

## Unit 4 — Matching Information + Summary with Options

**Mục tiêu:** Định vị thông tin theo chức năng đoạn; chọn phương án cho summary.  
**Độ dài:** ~720 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 12 · **Thời gian:** 22 phút.

### Reading passage

**Open Storage: The Limits of Museum Digitisation**

**A.** Most museum collections are invisible. Estimates vary, but in large institutions the proportion of objects on display is commonly between one and five per cent; the rest sits in climate-controlled rooms, catalogued but unseen. For most of the twentieth century, access depended on travel and on a letter of introduction. The rooms themselves are rarely visited, and rarely photographed. A collection that cannot be seen is, for most purposes, a collection that does not exist. Digitisation promised to change this: photograph or scan an object, publish the record, and anyone with a connection can study it. The promise was democratic — a student in a small town would see what a curator in a capital saw.

**B.** The work is slower and more expensive than the promise suggests. A single high-quality image requires controlled lighting, colour calibration and post-processing; a three-dimensional scan requires equipment and trained staff. A single manuscript may take a day to photograph and a week to describe. Each record also needs metadata — date, material, provenance, condition — much of which is missing from older catalogues. Funding usually arrives as grants for specific projects, so digitisation advances collection by collection, with the most photogenic objects first. Small museums with one photographer and no cataloguer fall further behind every year. A museum with a large digitisation team can produce tens of thousands of records a year; a regional one may manage a few hundred. A museum's online catalogue therefore reflects its funders' interests as much as its holdings.

**C.** Something is lost in translation. An image cannot convey weight, texture, smell, or the small asymmetries that reveal how an object was made and used. Residue analysis, wear patterns, the traces of an old repair: none survives photography. Conservation records, too, are often kept on paper and lost when a store is reorganised. A photograph also flattens scale, so that a colossal statue and a coin arrive at the same size on a screen. Researchers who once began by handling objects now begin with a screen, and some report that their questions have changed accordingly — from how an object was used to what it depicts. That is not a trivial shift; it is a change in what the collection is thought to be for.

**D.** Digitisation also produces discoveries. Cataloguing a collection of rings, a curator noticed that one labelled Roman had a setting consistent with nineteenth-century revival jewellery; the object was reclassified, and a small piece of the museum's own history was corrected. Bringing records together across institutions has revealed that fragments held in different countries belong to a single vessel. The gain is not only accuracy but the possibility of asking new questions of old material. Catalogues, unlike excavations, are cheap to revisit.

**E.** Access raises questions of ownership. Photographs of objects are new works with their own rights, and the institutions that hold them do not always hold those rights. Communities of origin may object to the circulation of images of sacred or funerary objects; some museums have restricted access, while others have been criticised for publishing first and consulting later. Some institutions now publish images with cultural protocols attached, restricting certain uses while permitting research. Copyright law, which differs from country to country, offers little guidance on cultural sensitivity.

**F.** The audience for digital collections is large but difficult to read. Online visitors vastly outnumber physical ones, yet "engagement" is measured in clicks and dwell time, which cannot distinguish a school project from a specialist's research. A museum that once counted visitors at the door now counts them in dozens of countries, and must decide what the number means. Curators report that digital access has increased demand for loans and visits rather than replacing them. The screen, in other words, has proved to be a shop window rather than a substitute for the building.

**G.** A digital catalogue is best understood as an index, not a collection. It should be funded as infrastructure — maintained, corrected and migrated to new systems — rather than as a series of spectacles that end when the grant does. And the metadata should be treated as part of an object's history: open to correction, attributed to the people who wrote it, and honest about what is missing. Metadata, unlike the objects, can be corrected by anyone with evidence.

### Reading passage

**The Fifteen-Minute City**

**A.** The "fifteen-minute city" is one of the few urban ideas of the past decade to have crossed from planning journals into ordinary conversation. Its claim is simple: residents should be able to reach six basic urban functions — living, working, commerce, healthcare, education and leisure — within a quarter of an hour on foot or by bicycle. The phrase was coined in 2016, and has since been attached to projects that share little beyond a preference for bicycles. The idea is associated with Paris, where the mayor's office adopted it in the years that followed and began reallocating road space, but its roots are older. It restates, in modern language, the ambition of the "neighbourhood unit" proposed by planners in the 1920s, and before that the dense, mixed districts that grew up before zoning separated homes from workplaces.

**B.** Its appeal lies partly in its modesty. Unlike the grand plans of the twentieth century, it does not require a city to be rebuilt; it requires streets to be rebalanced. A school, a clinic, a grocer and a park already exist in many districts; the question is whether residents can reach them without a car. The measure is not the distance to a facility but the quality of the journey to it. A supermarket within walking distance is of little use if the only route crosses a six-lane road. Data from European cities suggest that a large share of car journeys are shorter than three kilometres — distances that could plausibly be walked or cycled if the route felt safe and the destination had somewhere to leave a bicycle.

**C.** Trials have produced encouraging but partial results. In Melbourne, where a twenty-minute neighbourhood programme has run since 2017, residents of pilot areas reported walking more, although car use fell only slightly. The gap between intention and behaviour is familiar to transport researchers: people do not change how they travel because a map says they could. They change when parking becomes scarce, buses become frequent, and the walk feels pleasant. The same pattern appears in evaluations of cycle lanes, which are used far more when they are separated from traffic. Pilot areas are also chosen because they are likely to succeed, which flatters the results. Infrastructure, not information, does most of the work.

**D.** Critics raise three objections. The first is gentrification: a district that becomes pleasant to walk in becomes desirable to live in, and rents rise, displacing the residents the policy was meant to help. Rents, unlike bus routes, are not a matter of design. A shop that serves the neighbourhood may be replaced by one that serves visitors. The second is exclusion. The model assumes a mobile adult with time to spare; for a wheelchair user, a parent with a pram, or someone caring for an elderly relative, fifteen minutes on foot may be an optimistic fiction. The third concerns the geography of work. Many people commute across the city because that is where the jobs are, and making the local high street charming does not create local employment.

**E.** There is a political difficulty as well. Because the idea is easy to summarise, it has been easy to misrepresent; in several countries it has been presented as a scheme to confine residents to their districts, a claim with no basis in any policy document but considerable reach online. The lesson for city governments is that a plan which changes parking, traffic and street space will be resisted by some residents whatever it is called, and that consultation conducted after the barriers appear is not consultation. The barriers are visible while the benefits are slow, which makes the politics of the idea unusually difficult.

**F.** The most useful version of the fifteen-minute city is therefore modest: a diagnostic tool rather than a blueprint. It asks a city to map what people can actually reach, and to notice who cannot reach it. Measured that way, the idea is less about distance than about access — and access, unlike distance, can be improved by a bench, a crossing, or a bus that runs after dark. Cities that treat it as a slogan tend to produce photographs rather than bus routes. It is a way of asking better questions, not of answering them in advance.

**Giving Back the Past**

**A.** In November 2017, President Macron of France told an audience in Ouagadougou that he could not accept that a large part of African heritage was held in French museums. He commissioned a report from the academics Felwine Sarr and Bénédicte Savoy, which recommended that objects taken without consent during the colonial period be returned. Its central recommendation was blunt: where return was impossible, the claim should at least be acknowledged in public. The report did not begin the argument — museums had been receiving restitution claims for decades — but it gave the argument a new vocabulary and a timetable. Museums in France, Britain, Germany and Belgium had spent years insisting that their collections were legally untouchable, and the report gave politicians a way of saying otherwise. Restitution moved from a moral demand made by governments to a practical question asked by institutions.

**B.** The case for return rests on provenance. Many objects in European collections were acquired through military expeditions, punitive raids or exchanges so unequal that consent is difficult to establish. The Benin Bronzes, taken by British forces in 1897, are the most cited example; Ethiopia's Maqdala treasures, seized in 1868, are another. Ownership, in this account, is not a neutral fact but a relationship that can be broken. For source communities, the issue is not only legal title but the loss of objects that carry religious and historical meaning. A mask in a vitrine is, in this view, an object kept away from the ceremonies that give it sense.

**C.** The case against is made less confidently than it once was, but it has not vanished. Directors of universal museums argue that their collections allow objects from different cultures to be seen side by side, and that this comparison is itself a public good. They point to conservation, security and the risk that returned objects may be displayed in less stable conditions — an argument that critics regard as condescending, since it assumes that source countries cannot look after their own heritage. Legal obstacles are real: the British Museum Act of 1963 restricts the disposal of objects from the national collection, so returning them would require legislation rather than a decision by trustees. The law, in other words, was written to protect collections from being sold, not to settle questions of colonial justice. Trustees, meanwhile, are bound by the law that created them, even when they find it embarrassing.

**D.** In practice, the debate has produced a range of arrangements rather than a single solution. Long-term loans, joint ownership, rotating displays and shared digital archives allow objects to travel without a permanent transfer of title. In 2022 Germany agreed to transfer ownership of a large number of Benin Bronzes to Nigeria, with some objects remaining on loan in German museums. Ownership, once transferred, can be loaned back on terms that both sides can defend at home. Each agreement tends to be bespoke, which makes them slow and difficult to copy. Such deals are criticised from both sides — as too little by campaigners and as a surrender by some donors — but they have the advantage of being achievable within existing law.

**E.** Restitution is not only about objects. It concerns records, photographs, excavation notes and, most sensitive of all, human remains. The return of remains raises questions that antiquities do not: communities may ask for reburial rather than display, and the appropriate response may be to stop treating the material as a collection item at all. For communities, the return of a relative's remains is not an act of heritage policy but of mourning. The question of who speaks for a community is itself contested, and museums that ask it discover that there is rarely a single answer. Several institutions have removed such material from public view while they consult, a process that has sometimes taken years.

**F.** The debate also exposes a tension inside the idea of the universal museum. Universality was once used to justify keeping: the museum claimed to belong to the world, and therefore to be the best custodian of everyone's objects. The same principle can now be used to justify returning them, since a truly universal collection might be one distributed across many cities. Universality, in other words, is a claim that can be turned against the institutions that made it. Institutions that resist may find their legitimacy, and eventually their funding, questioned by the publics they claim to serve.

**G.** What has changed, above all, is the question. A generation ago it was whether objects should go back. It is now how, on what terms, and with what continuing relationship. That is a less dramatic question and a more demanding one, because it requires money, legislation, scholars who can describe what was taken, and the patience to negotiate agreements that will look imperfect to everyone involved. It is a slower kind of justice, and possibly a more durable one. The alternative — waiting for a perfect settlement — has already consumed several decades. The museums that emerge well from this period will probably be those that treated restitution not as a loss but as a redefinition of what a collection is for.
