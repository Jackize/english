# C2 Listening — IELTS Academic 8.5–9.0

**Level:** C2 (CEFR) · **IELTS Academic:** 8.5–9.0 · **Số unit:** 5  
**Kỹ năng:** natural-speed lectures, stance/inference, accents/reductions, complex note-taking, full mock  
**Ngôn ngữ:** hướng dẫn tiếng Việt; script, câu hỏi và đáp án tiếng Anh

> Ở C2, khó khăn không còn là “nghe không kịp từ”. Khó khăn là **nghe được lập trường**: người nói đang khẳng định, đang nghi ngờ, đang nhượng bộ hay đang mỉa mai? Bài nghe dài hơn, tốc độ tự nhiên hơn, và đáp án thường nằm ở **quan hệ giữa các câu**, không ở một từ khóa.

## 0. Giới thiệu & cách học

### 0.1 Hồ sơ người nghe C2

- Nghe lecture 500–650 từ ở tốc độ 150–170 từ/phút, hiểu mạch lập luận.
- Nhận ra signposting, hedging, self-correction, concession và irony.
- Ghi chú bằng ký hiệu, không cố viết cả câu.
- Phân biệt **main claim** với **example**, **necessary** với **sufficient**.
- Nghe được biến thể Anh–Mỹ–Úc và các dạng giảm âm phổ biến.
- Không bị lừa bởi distractor xuất hiện trước khi người nói sửa lại.

### 0.2 Quy trình 3 lần nghe

| Lần | Mục đích | Cách làm |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Gist | Nắm chủ đề, lập trường, cấu trúc | Nghe trọn, không dừng; trả lời 2–3 câu gist |
| 2 — Detail | Nghe chi tiết, quan hệ, số liệu | Dừng sau 2–3 câu; điền note/MCQ |
| 3 — Shadowing | Nghe âm, nhịp, ngắt cụm | Đọc đuổi; đánh dấu giảm âm, nối âm |

### Script

> Good afternoon. Today I want to examine something that is often treated as a technical issue but is, I think, fundamentally rhetorical: the language through which expertise is asserted. My argument is not that experts are dishonest. It is that the authority of expertise is performed through specific linguistic moves, and that recognising those moves changes how we should read expert claims.
>
> Let me begin with a familiar scene. A government minister stands at a podium and says, “The science is clear.” The sentence sounds like a description of reality. But it is doing something else as well: it is closing a debate. It implies that anyone who disagrees is not merely wrong but irrational. Now, sometimes the science is clear. But the phrase does not tell us which science, whose evidence, or what uncertainties have been omitted. It is a rhetorical shortcut, and like all shortcuts, it can be useful or misleading.
>
> The first move I want to identify is hedging. Experts often qualify their claims: “the evidence suggests”, “it is likely that”, “under these conditions”. This is sometimes seen as weakness, but it is usually a mark of competence. It signals the limits of the evidence. The problem is that public discourse often strips hedging away. A cautious report becomes a headline that says “X causes Y”, and the qualification disappears.
>
> The second move is citation. Citing a study can function as evidence, but it can also function as a shield. “Studies show” is not an argument; it is an appeal to an unnamed authority. The question we should ask is not simply whether a study exists, but whether it is relevant, whether it has been replicated, and whether the person citing it has understood its limits.
>
> The third move is concession. A skilled expert will often say, “There is something in the opposing view, but...” This is not weakness either. It is a way of showing that the argument has been tested. The danger is ceremonial concession: acknowledging an objection in order to dismiss it without answering it. You can usually tell the difference by asking whether the concession changes the conclusion.
>
> So what follows from this? Not that we should distrust expertise. That would be naive, and it would leave us at the mercy of whoever shouts loudest. The point is that expertise should be judged by its warrants, not just its confidence. A claim that explains its limits is more trustworthy than one that does not. And a public culture that rewards certainty over calibration will get more certainty, but not more knowledge.

### Script

> Today’s lecture is about memory consolidation, and I want to start by correcting a misconception. Many people assume that memory is like a recording: an experience happens, the brain stores it, and later you play it back. That model is intuitive, but it is wrong in an important way. Memory is reconstructive. What you retrieve is not a fixed copy of the past; it is a reconstruction shaped by prior knowledge, expectations and the context of retrieval.
>
> So what happens during consolidation? Broadly, experiences are encoded in a fragile form, and over time they are stabilised and integrated with existing knowledge. Sleep plays a role here, but the story is more complicated than “sleep improves memory”. Different stages of sleep appear to support different kinds of consolidation. Deep sleep seems important for declarative memory — facts and events — while REM sleep may support procedural and emotional learning. The evidence is not uniform, and I would be cautious about simple prescriptions.
>
> There is also a trade-off. Consolidation is not simply about preserving more. It is about selecting what to keep and what to discard. Forgetting is not always a failure; it can be adaptive, because it reduces interference and allows generalisation. A system that remembered everything would be overwhelmed. So when we say we want to “improve memory”, we should ask: which memories, for what purpose, and at what cost?
>
> Retrieval is equally important. The act of retrieving a memory can strengthen it, but it can also distort it. Each time you recall an event, you may incorporate new information, and the memory can become more confident without becoming more accurate. This has practical implications. Eyewitness testimony, for example, is susceptible to contamination through repeated questioning. The confidence of a witness is not a reliable guide to accuracy.
>
> Finally, I want to qualify the idea that memory is a single system. It is better understood as a set of systems that interact. Declarative memory, procedural skill, emotional memory and working memory have different properties and different neural bases. A deficit in one does not necessarily imply a deficit in another. This matters for education: a student who struggles to recall facts may be perfectly capable of learning a procedure, and vice versa. The practical lesson is to assess the specific system, not the global label.

### Script

> I want to talk this evening about why forecasts fail, and why the failure is often less interesting than the way forecasts are presented. Let me be clear at the outset: I am not arguing that forecasting is useless. Weather forecasts, for example, are remarkably good. The issue is that the authority of a forecast is often borrowed from its precision, rather than from its track record.
>
> Consider a simple distinction. A forecast can be accurate but not useful, or useful but not accurate. If I tell you it will rain somewhere in the country tomorrow, I am likely to be accurate, but you cannot act on it. If I tell you it will rain on your street at 3 p.m., I am useful, but probably wrong. Good forecasting is a trade-off between resolution and reliability, and the trade-off is rarely made explicit.
>
> The second problem is the treatment of uncertainty. Models produce ranges, probabilities and scenarios, but public communication often collapses them into a single number. A 60% chance of rain becomes “it will rain”. A projected range of 1.5 to 2.5 degrees becomes “2 degrees”. This is not necessarily dishonesty; it is a response to the way media and politics demand certainty. But it creates a calibration problem: people learn to trust precise numbers, and then lose trust when the numbers are wrong.
>
> The third problem is structural. Forecasts are often built on assumptions that are stable in normal times but break down in crises. A model trained on historical data may fail precisely when the future is most different from the past. This is not a reason to abandon models; it is a reason to stress-test them and to be explicit about their scope.
>
> So what should we do? I would suggest three things. First, report uncertainty in ways that are usable, not just technically correct. Second, evaluate forecasts by their track record, not their confidence. Third, distinguish between predictions that are robust across models and those that depend on a single set of assumptions. The most dangerous forecast is not the one that is wrong; it is the one that is presented as certain and then treated as a fact.
