# C1 Listening — Lecture Argument, Stance & Section 4 Density

**Level:** C1 (CEFR) · **IELTS Academic:** 7.5–8.0 · **Số unit:** 6 (5 bài học + 1 mock)  
**Trọng tâm:** cấu trúc lecture, signposting, stance & hedging, inference, note-taking dưới tốc độ, accent & connected speech, distractor–paraphrase, Section 4 density  
**Ngôn ngữ:** hướng dẫn tiếng Việt; script, câu hỏi và đáp án tiếng Anh

> Ở C1, điểm không còn đến từ việc nghe được từ. Điểm đến từ việc **theo được lập luận**: người nói đang chứng minh gì, giới hạn gì, phản đối ai. Bài giảng thật hiếm khi nói thẳng "đây là quan điểm của tôi"; họ nói *"it would be premature to..."*, rồi tự sửa giữa câu. Năm bài dưới đây luyện đúng tầng nghĩa đó. Mọi script là bài viết gốc.

## 0. Giới thiệu & cách học

### 0.1 Hồ sơ nghe C1

Band 7.5–8.0 thường cần khoảng **32–34/40** và **35–36/40** ở bài thật. Nhưng mục tiêu không phải con số, mà là những năng lực tạo ra nó:

- Nghe lecture 5–6 phút, tốc độ 150–170 từ/phút, giữ được **dàn bài** và xử lý **Section 4 density**.
- Phân biệt **claim**, **evidence**, **qualification**.
- Nhận ra **stance**: tin, nghi ngờ, tiếc nuối, phản đối, hay chỉ đang thuật lại quan điểm người khác.
- Nhận ra **hedge**, **self-correction** và **paraphrase xa**: *largely, sorry — the twelfth*, *what they were for*.
- Xử lý **Section 4 density**: hai ý trong một câu, số liệu kèm đơn vị.
- Nghe được Anh, Mỹ, Úc, Scotland, Ấn Độ; chịu được reduction, elision, assimilation.

Lỗi C1 thường gặp là **hiểu đúng từ, sai chức năng**: nghe *"You might assume..."* rồi ghi đó là quan điểm của giảng viên, trong khi ý đó sắp bị bác bỏ.

### 0.2 Kiến trúc một bài giảng

| Phần | Chức năng | Dấu hiệu | Ghi gì |
|---|---|---|---|
| Framing | Đặt vấn đề | *Today I want to...*, *We tend to assume...* | Chủ đề + lý do |
| Claim | Luận điểm chính | *My argument is...* | 1 câu |
| Roadmap | Báo trước các phần | *I'll make three moves...* | 2–4 nhánh |
| Sections | Thân bài | *Let me start with...*, *That brings me to...* | Keyword từng phần |
| Recapitulation | Tóm lại | *To draw the threads together...* | Câu chốt |
| Evaluation | Giới hạn, phản biện | *I'd want to be careful...*, *That said...* | Điều bị giới hạn |

Mỗi phần thường theo **problem → solution**, **chronology**, **compare–contrast** hoặc **taxonomy**. Biết kiểu nào đang chạy, bạn biết nên chờ *"The second type..."* hay *"By contrast..."*.

### 0.3 Signposting và stance

| Chức năng | Cụm điển hình | Cần nghe gì |
|---|---|---|
| Mở đầu | *take a familiar object and make it strange* | Chủ đề sẽ bị soi lại |
| Lộ trình | *I'll make three moves* | Đếm đủ ba phần |
| Tự sửa | *or rather*, *sorry*, *I mean* | Đáp án nằm sau |
| Hedge | *largely*, *tends to*, *it would be too strong to say* | Đừng ghi thành quy luật tuyệt đối |
| Phản biện nhẹ | *There's something in that, but...* | Đồng ý một phần rồi bác |
| Caveat cuối | *a caution about my own argument* | Điểm hay bị hỏi inference |

Ba loại stance: **epistemic** (mức chắc chắn: *clearly, probably*), **evaluative** (tốt/xấu: *crude, misleading*), **attributional** (quan điểm của ai: *critics argue, the standard account runs like this*). Bỏ qua attribution, bạn sẽ gán quan điểm người khác cho giảng viên.

## Unit 1 — Cấu trúc lecture & signposting (humanities)

**Mục tiêu:** Dựng dàn bài ba phần từ roadmap; nhận ra chỗ giảng viên tự giới hạn phạm vi.  
**Script:** 536 từ · 1 người nói · 150–160 từ/phút.

### Vocabulary

| Word/phrase | IPA | Nghĩa |
|---|---|---|
| historiography | /ˌhɪstɒriˈɒɡrəfi/ | sử học, cách viết sử |
| threshold | /ˈθreʃhəʊld/ | ngưỡng, bậc cửa |
| caveat | /ˈkæviæt/ | lời cảnh báo, giới hạn |

### External sources

- **British Council — C1 Listening:** https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/listening/c1-listening — bài học thuật có câu hỏi attitude.
- **Gresham College:** https://www.gresham.ac.uk/ — public lecture về *history, cities*; chỉ ghi roadmap.

### Script

> Good afternoon. What I want to do today is take a familiar object — the city street — and make it strange. My argument, put bluntly, is that the street is not simply the space left over between buildings. It is a social institution; and like any institution, it has been designed, contested and, in some places, deliberately dismantled. I'll make three moves. First, I'll sketch how historians came to treat the street as evidence. Second, I'll examine one case: the disappearance of the doorstep as a social site. And third, I'll suggest why the language we use for streets in policy documents matters more than we usually admit. One caveat before I begin: most of my examples are European, and I'll return to that limitation at the end.
>
> Let me start with the historiography. It would be wrong to say that earlier historians ignored streets — they mapped them meticulously, street by street, sometimes house by house. What changed in the 1960s was not the attention but the question. Instead of asking how streets were laid out, scholars began asking what they were for. Once you ask that, the street stops being a container and becomes a set of practices: loitering, trading, greeting, arguing, waiting. The evidence becomes correspondingly awkward — diaries, photographs, court records, novels — none of it designed to answer the question we are putting to it. So the first thing to notice is that this is a history written against the grain of its sources.
>
> My second move takes me to the doorstep. In the industrial districts of the late nineteenth century, the doorstep was a threshold in almost the anthropological sense. It was not private, exactly, and not public either. Neighbours sat on it; children played around it; news was exchanged across it. It belonged to the household, but it faced the street, and that doubleness made it socially useful. Slum clearance and the shift to apartment blocks did not simply move people; they removed the architectural feature that made this casual sociability possible. I'm simplifying, of course: in some cities the doorstep survived, and in others the balcony took over its function. But the general point holds. When we mourn the loss of "community", we are often mourning a lost piece of design.
>
> That brings me to my third and, for today, final move: policy language. Contemporary documents are full of words like vibrant, liveable and walkable. These look like descriptions; in fact they are evaluations wearing the costume of measurements. A street can be measured for width and footfall, but not for vibrancy. My concern is not that the words are vague — vagueness can be honest — but that they conceal a decision about who a street is for.
>
> So, to draw the threads together: the street is an institution, its sociability is designed into or out of it, and the way we describe it is never neutral. As for my European examples — that is not a trivial caveat. Theories of public space developed in one region have been exported wholesale, and they have not always travelled well.

**Mục tiêu:** Phân biệt điều giảng viên tin, điều bà ấy thuật lại, và mức chắc chắn của từng claim.

| tolerable | /ˈtɒlərəbl/ | có thể chấp nhận được |

### External sources

- **TED Talks:** https://www.ted.com/talks — tìm *risk, trust*; ghi 3 hedge và 1 chỗ tự sửa.
- **BBC — 6 Minute English:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish/english/features/6-minute-english — tập về *risk, decision-making*.

### Script

> Today I want to talk about risk — not the technical measurement of it, but the strange gap between the risks experts describe and the risks people worry about. I should declare my position at the outset, because it shapes everything that follows. I don't think the gap is a failure of public understanding. I think it is largely a rational response to institutions that have not always deserved trust. That is a stronger claim than the evidence can carry on its own, so let me qualify it as I go.
>
> The standard account, which you'll find in most textbooks, runs roughly like this. Lay people are said to be poor at probability. They overestimate rare, dramatic hazards — plane crashes, terrorism, nuclear accidents — and underestimate common ones — driving, diet, household falls. The usual explanation is what psychologists call availability: dramatic events are easier to recall, so they feel more likely. That is a plausible mechanism, and there is experimental support for it. On this view, the task of communication is to correct the error, usually by supplying numbers.
>
> I want to suggest that this account is not so much wrong as incomplete, and that it mistakes a symptom for a cause. Consider trust. When a regulator says a chemical is safe, the public is being asked to accept a conclusion without seeing the reasoning. If that regulator has previously been wrong, or has appeared to side with industry, the scepticism is not irrational; it is Bayesian. People are updating on the institution's track record, not failing to understand the statistic.
>
> There's a second issue: control. Risks we choose feel different from risks imposed on us. The same objective probability can be tolerable or intolerable depending on whether you consented to it. This is why seatbelt laws were accepted and fluoridation was fought, though the evidence for both was strong.
>
> Now, I'd want to be careful here. It would be too strong to say that knowledge is irrelevant; plainly it isn't. People who understand probability do make different judgements. But expertise is not the only thing that matters, and treating it as the only thing is itself a kind of bias. Let me put it more precisely: the deficit model — the idea that the public simply lacks facts — explains less than we once thought.
>
> What follows for policy? Three modest proposals. First, communicate uncertainty rather than suppressing it; a range is more honest than a point estimate. Second, publish the reasoning, not just the verdict, so that people can audit the conclusion. Third, treat dissent as data — a signal about where trust has broken, not merely noise to be managed. None of this is costless: communicating uncertainty is slower, and it is much harder to put on a poster.
>
> I'll end with a caution about my own argument. It is comfortable for academics to conclude that the public is rational; it flatters the audience and it costs us nothing. But some worries really are disproportionate, and saying otherwise can be a way of avoiding the harder question of who bears the cost of being wrong.

## Unit 3 — Note-taking dưới tốc độ (science/technology)

**Mục tiêu:** Ghi được cơ chế và con số khi bài giảng dày đặc, không cố viết câu.  
**Script:** 520 từ · 1 người nói · 155–170 từ/phút.

### Vocabulary

| Word/phrase | IPA | Nghĩa |
|---|---|---|
| thermal storage | /ˈθɜːml ˈstɔːrɪdʒ/ | lưu trữ nhiệt |
| sensible heat | /ˈsensəbl hiːt/ | nhiệt hiện (làm tăng nhiệt độ) |
| latent heat | /ˈleɪtnt hiːt/ | nhiệt ẩn (khi chuyển pha) |
| round-trip efficiency | /ˌraʊnd ˈtrɪp ɪˈfɪʃnsi/ | hiệu suất nạp–xả |

### External sources

- **TED-Ed:** https://ed.ted.com/ — video ngắn về *energy, batteries*; ghi 5 số liệu kèm đơn vị.
- **BBC Learning English:** https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish/ — *6 Minute English* về *renewable energy*; chép 3 câu có số.

### Script

> Right — I'm going to move quickly, because there's a lot of ground. Don't try to write full sentences; the slides are online and the numbers are on the handout. What I want to explain is why thermal storage, which sounds like a technology from the 1970s, is suddenly interesting again.
>
> Start with the problem. Electricity demand is not flat, and a grid with a lot of wind and solar has periods of surplus and periods of scarcity. Batteries are excellent at shifting energy across hours, but much less good at shifting it across weeks. And the seasonal gap is the hard one: in northern Europe, solar output in December may be a tenth of what it is in June, while demand peaks. The key variable is not how much energy you can store, but how quickly you can get it back.
>
> There are three broad ways to store heat. The first is sensible heat: you heat a material and keep it hot. Water is cheap and holds about 4.2 kilojoules per kilogram per degree. The second is latent heat: you use the energy absorbed when a material changes phase, usually solid to liquid. Ice stores cold; molten salt stores heat. Latent systems are more compact than water tanks, but they need careful engineering because the material expands as it melts. The third is thermochemical: you drive a reversible reaction and get the heat back later. That last one has the highest theoretical density and the least mature engineering. The categories are not rivals; a well-designed plant may combine two of them.
>
> Now, sand. Sand is abundant, cheap, and stable to very high temperatures — well above 500 degrees, in some designs above 1,000. It is ordinary construction sand, not a rare material, which is why the idea appeals to engineers working on cost rather than prestige. A sand battery is not a battery in the electrochemical sense. It's an insulated silo filled with sand, with electric heaters embedded in it and a heat exchanger to draw energy out. Finland has one of the best-known installations; it charges when electricity is cheap and discharges hot air into a district heating network. Round-trip efficiency is lower than a lithium-ion battery — you lose perhaps a quarter of the electricity on the way in and out — but the capital cost is a fraction, and the storage medium doesn't degrade after 5,000 cycles. It is, in that sense, less a breakthrough than a return to first principles.
>
> Three caveats, quickly. First, this stores heat, not electricity; it is only useful where there is a demand for heat. Second, the economics depend on the price difference between cheap and expensive hours, which policy can change overnight. Third — and this is the one I'd underline — a sand silo is not a solution to seasonal storage on its own. The losses are too large over months. It is a tool for days, not seasons.
>

### Script

> I want to begin with a number that gets quoted a lot and is almost always misunderstood. Somewhere between 60 and 80 per cent of the clothes donated to charities in Europe and North America are not sold in the shop where they were donated. They are baled and exported — to Ghana, Kenya, Pakistan, Chile, the United Arab Emirates. The figure is often presented as proof of waste, or of hypocrisy. I want to suggest that it is proof of something more specific: that the system is doing exactly what it was designed to do, and that the problem lies in the design.
>
> Let's be precise about the flow. A charity shop is not a recycling centre; it is a retail operation whose surplus is a commodity. Sorting is done by weight and quality, not by sentiment. The best items go to the shop floor; the rest are graded. Grade one goes to Eastern Europe, grade two to Africa, grade three is sold as wiping cloths or shredded for insulation. Nobody is being deceived at the point of donation, exactly, but nobody is being told where the bag goes either.
>
> Now, the effects in importing countries. The standard criticism is that cheap second-hand clothing destroys local textile industries. There's something in that, but the causality is messier than the slogan suggests. In Ghana, the decline of local manufacturing coincided with structural adjustment policies, currency liberalisation and, yes, import liberalisation. Second-hand clothing was one factor among several, and in some periods it was a minor one. To say it destroyed the industry is to give it more explanatory power than the evidence supports.
>
> A second criticism is environmental. Here the arithmetic is genuinely awkward. A cotton T-shirt has already absorbed a large quantity of water and energy by the time it reaches a charity shop; extending its life avoids new production. On that measure, reuse is clearly better than recycling, and both are better than landfill. But the export leg adds transport, and in destination countries the unsellable fraction — maybe a third of what arrives — often ends up burned or in informal dumps. So the net benefit depends on what happens at the end of the chain, which is precisely where data are weakest.
>
> Three figures, since you'll want them. Globally, we produce something like 92 million tonnes of textile waste a year. Less than one per cent of collected clothing is recycled into new fibres. And the average garment is worn between seven and ten times before it is discarded.
>
> What would a better system look like? Not a ban on exports, which would remove an income stream from precisely the people the ban claims to protect. Rather: quality sorting at source, extended producer responsibility, and — this is the unglamorous part — investment in local repair and resale infrastructure in importing countries.
>
> I began with a statistic. I'd like to end by questioning the way we use it. "Sixty per cent is exported" is not a scandal in itself. The scandal is that no one is required to know what happens next.

> Good morning. Today I want to explain how a philosophical abstraction — time — became a piece of infrastructure. My argument is simple: standard time was not discovered; it was negotiated. And the negotiations were as much political as scientific.

>
> The railways solved the problem by imposing their own time. In 1840 the Great Western Railway adopted London time across its whole network. Other companies followed, and by 1847 the Railway Clearing House had recommended it for all of them. But adoption was uneven. For years, some station clocks carried two minute hands — one for railway time, one for local time — so that passengers could see both. Notice that the conference did not choose a time system; it chose a reference point. France abstained, and kept Paris time until 1911. So even the country that hosted the metric system resisted for nearly thirty years.
>
> Why does this matter? Because standard time made possible things we now take for granted: coordinated transport, shift work, the appointment, the deadline. It also created a new kind of discipline. When the factory clock and the town clock agree, lateness becomes a measurable offence.
>

4. **Ôn đúng chỗ yếu:** sai số liệu → chép chính tả số; sai inference → luyện stance; sai paraphrase → viết cặp audio–câu hỏi.

**Lịch 4 tuần:** mỗi tuần 1 unit mới + 1 unit cũ; 20 phút/ngày; 5 câu dictation/ngày; cuối tuần làm mock hoặc nghe một lecture ngoài 10 phút.

**Nguồn ngoài:** link là cấp trang/chuyên mục, không phải link sâu. Nếu link đổi, vào trang chủ và tìm theo từ khóa trong mô tả. Không tải hoặc đăng lại audio, transcript của nguồn khác.
