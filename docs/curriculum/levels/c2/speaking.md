## Topic 1 — Society & Trust: Fluency, Stance, Extended Turns

**Mục tiêu:** Nói dài có mạch về một chủ đề xã hội; thể hiện stance bằng ngôn ngữ có mức độ; dùng ví dụ cụ thể.  
**Thời lượng:** 45 phút.

### Part 1 questions

1. Do you know many of your neighbours?
2. Would you say your neighbourhood is a trusting place?
3. Has the way people interact in your city changed since you were a child?
4. Do you think people trust strangers more or less than before?
5. What makes you trust someone you have just met?
6. Is trust important in your work or studies?

### Useful language

| Cụm | Nghĩa | Ví dụ |
|---|---|---|
| a sense of trust | cảm giác tin cậy | *There’s a strong sense of trust in my building.* |
| take people at face value | tin người ngay từ đầu | *I tend to take people at face value.* |
| earn someone’s trust | giành được tin cậy | *Trust has to be earned over time.* |
| a low-trust environment | môi trường ít tin cậy | *A low-trust environment makes cooperation costly.* |
| social fabric | kết cấu xã hội | *Small interactions hold the social fabric together.* |
| on balance | nhìn chung | *On balance, I think people are more cautious.* |
| I’m inclined to think | tôi nghiêng về | *I’m inclined to think trust has declined.* |
| it would be misleading to | sẽ gây hiểu nhầm nếu | *It would be misleading to say trust has vanished.* |
| a necessary condition | điều kiện cần | *Contact is a necessary condition for trust.* |
| not sufficient | chưa đủ | *Contact alone is not sufficient.* |
| to some extent | ở một mức độ nào đó | *To some extent, technology has helped.* |
| the flip side | mặt trái | *The flip side is greater suspicion.* |
| a self-fulfilling prophecy | lời tiên tri tự ứng nghiệm | *Distrust can become a self-fulfilling prophecy.* |
| in practice | trên thực tế | *In practice, trust is local.* |
| than is often assumed | hơn người ta thường nghĩ | *Trust matters more than is often assumed.* |

### Part 2 cue card

> Describe a time when you had to trust someone you did not know well.
>
> You should say:
> - who the person was
> - what the situation was
> - why you had to trust them
> - and explain how you felt afterwards.

### Outline

| Phần | Nội dung |
|---|---|
| Who/when | a landlord, two years ago, moving to a new flat |
| Situation | paid a deposit before seeing the contract; keys handed over later |
| Why trust | recommended by a friend; no agency; time pressure |
| Feeling | anxious, then relieved; trust was repaid but not guaranteed |

### Model answer

> I had to trust a landlord I barely knew when I moved into my current flat about two years ago. I’d found the place through a friend of a friend, and everything was arranged rather quickly. The landlord asked for a deposit before I had seen the full contract, which, in retrospect, was not the most sensible thing to agree to. But I was under pressure to move, the flat was exactly what I needed, and he came across as straightforward.
>
> What made the situation uncomfortable was that I had no institutional protection — no agency, no standard form, just a bank transfer and a promise. I remember handing over the money and thinking, “I have absolutely no leverage here.” He could have disappeared, changed the terms, or delayed the keys. In the end, he did none of those things. He sent the contract the next day, met me at the flat, and even helped me carry a few boxes.
>
> Looking back, I think the experience taught me two things. First, trust is often a calculated risk rather than a feeling; I trusted him because the cost of not doing so was high and the signals were reasonably good. Second, the fact that it worked out doesn’t prove that it was wise. I was lucky as much as I was perceptive. These days, I’d insist on seeing the contract first — not because I’ve become cynical, but because good systems should not depend on strangers being virtuous.

### Part 3 questions

1. Why do some communities have higher levels of trust than others?
2. Can trust be rebuilt once it has been broken?
3. Is it possible to have a well-functioning society without high levels of trust?

### Model answers

> **Why do some communities have higher levels of trust than others?**
>
> I suspect the answer has less to do with culture than with institutions and repeated interaction. Where people expect to deal with the same neighbours, shopkeepers and officials over many years, they have reasons to behave decently: reputation matters, and cheating is costly. Where populations are highly mobile and transactions are one-off, the incentives are different. That said, institutions matter too — if contracts are enforced predictably and corruption is low, people can afford to trust strangers because they do not need to rely on goodwill alone. So I’d say trust is partly a product of stability and partly a product of enforceable rules. It would be misleading to treat it as a fixed national characteristic.

> **Can trust be rebuilt once it has been broken?**
>
> Yes, but not by reassurance alone. If an institution has covered up a failure, for example, saying “trust us” is almost counterproductive; what matters is acknowledgment, transparency and a change in the incentives that produced the failure. Rebuilding trust is therefore slow and procedural. It requires allowing independent scrutiny, accepting that some claims will be met with scepticism, and behaving consistently over time. On a personal level, the same is broadly true: an apology helps, but what really rebuilds trust is a pattern of behaviour that makes the promise credible. I’m inclined to think trust is repaired through structure more than through sentiment.

### Pronunciation & prosody

- **Sentence stress:** *I had NO institutional protection* — nhấn *no*; *I was LUCKY as much as I was perceptive* — nhấn cặp đối lập.
- **Chunking:** *What made the situation uncomfortable / was that I had no institutional protection.*
- **Intonation:** liệt kê *no agency, no standard form, just a bank transfer and a promise* — xuống giọng ở mỗi mục, lên nhẹ trước *and*.
- **Weak forms:** *had to* /ˈhæd tə/, *could have* /ˈkʊd əv/.

### Common Vietnamese mistakes

| ❌ Sai | ✅ Đúng | Vì sao |
|---|---|---|
| *I very trust him.* | *I trusted him a great deal.* | *Very* không đứng trước động từ. |
| *He is a person who I can believe.* | *He is someone I can trust.* | *Believe* cần *in* hoặc *believe someone*, nhưng *trust* tự nhiên hơn. |
| *It depends on the culture of that place.* | *It depends on local norms and institutions.* | Tránh *culture* mơ hồ. |
| *I was boring.* | *I was bored.* | *Boring* = gây chán; *bored* = cảm thấy chán. |

## Topic 2 — Ethics & Technology: Abstraction, Hypotheticals, Concession

**Mục tiêu:** Thảo luận đạo đức công nghệ; dùng câu điều kiện, hypothetical, concession; tránh cảm tính.  
**Thời lượng:** 50 phút.

### Part 1 questions

1. How often do you use artificial intelligence in your daily life?
2. Do you think AI tools make people more or less creative?
3. Would you trust an AI to give you medical advice?
4. Should companies be allowed to use personal data to train AI?
5. Do you think children should learn about AI at school?
6. What technology could you not live without?

### Useful language

| Cụm | Nghĩa | Ví dụ |
|---|---|---|
| a trade-off | sự đánh đổi | *There’s a trade-off between convenience and control.* |
| at stake | điều đang bị ảnh hưởng | *What is at stake is not just privacy.* |
| a precautionary approach | cách tiếp cận phòng ngừa | *A precautionary approach is justified.* |
| meaningful consent | sự đồng ý có ý nghĩa | *Users rarely give meaningful consent.* |
| accountability | trách nhiệm giải trình | *Accountability is difficult when systems are opaque.* |
| by design | ngay từ thiết kế | *Privacy should be protected by design.* |
| a false dichotomy | lưỡng phân sai | *Safety versus innovation is a false dichotomy.* |
| it would be naive to | sẽ ngây thơ nếu | *It would be naive to assume companies self-regulate.* |
| the burden of proof | nghĩa vụ chứng minh | *The burden of proof should fall on the developer.* |
| irreversible harm | tổn hại không thể đảo ngược | *We should be cautious about irreversible harm.* |
| in principle / in practice | về nguyên tắc / thực tế | *In principle, users can opt out; in practice, they don’t.* |
| to the extent that | trong chừng mực | *To the extent that systems affect rights, they need oversight.* |
| a necessary safeguard | biện pháp bảo vệ cần thiết | *Auditing is a necessary safeguard.* |
| not a sufficient one | chưa đủ | *Transparency is necessary but not sufficient.* |
| morally defensible | có thể biện minh về đạo đức | *The policy is morally defensible only if...* |

### Part 2 cue card

> Describe a technology you think has created new ethical problems.
>
> You should say:
> - what the technology is
> - how it is used
> - what problems it creates
> - and explain what should be done about them.

### Outline

| Phần | Nội dung |
|---|---|
| Technology | facial recognition |
| Use | security, unlocking phones, public surveillance |
| Problems | consent, bias, chilling effect, false matches |
| Solution | strict limits, audit, warrants, right to appeal |

### Model answer

> I’d like to talk about facial recognition, because it’s a technology that has moved from science fiction into everyday life remarkably quickly. It’s used to unlock phones, verify identities at borders and, in some cities, monitor public spaces. The convenience is obvious: you don’t have to remember a password, and a camera can process thousands of faces in minutes.
>
> The ethical problems, though, are quite serious. The first is consent. If you walk down a street, you can’t meaningfully agree to have your face analysed, and you may not even know it’s happening. The second is accuracy. Studies have shown that some systems perform less well on certain demographic groups, which means the people who are already over-policed may bear the highest risk of false matches. The third is subtler: even if the system is accurate, the knowledge that you are being watched can change how you behave. That’s a political effect, not just a technical one.
>
> What should be done? I don’t think a blanket ban is realistic, because there are legitimate uses, such as finding a missing child. But I would restrict the technology to specific, high-stakes purposes, require independent auditing and a warrant for public-space use, and give people a right to know when a decision has been made by a machine. The burden of proof should fall on the organisation deploying the system, not on the public. Convenience is not a sufficient justification for infrastructure that can track everyone.

### Part 3 questions

1. Should developers be held responsible for the harmful uses of their technology?
2. Is it possible to regulate AI without slowing innovation?
3. Do people understand the ethical implications of the technology they use?
4. Should there be a global ban on autonomous weapons?
5. How can governments make AI decisions accountable?
6. Is privacy becoming obsolete?

### Model answers

> **Should developers be held responsible for the harmful uses of their technology?**
>
> I think responsibility should be distributed rather than concentrated on one actor. Developers cannot foresee every use, and holding them liable for any downstream harm would chill innovation. But they are not neutral either: design choices — what data to collect, what defaults to set, what warnings to give — shape the range of possible uses. So the reasonable position is that developers owe a duty of care for foreseeable harms and should be required to document risks. The user who deploys the system in a reckless way also bears responsibility. The difficult cases are those where harm is foreseeable but diffuse, and that is precisely where regulation should allocate the burden explicitly rather than leaving it to litigation.

> **Is it possible to regulate AI without slowing innovation?**
>
> It’s possible, but the framing is misleading. Some regulation will slow some innovation, and that may be desirable if the innovation is harmful. The real question is whether the rules impose disproportionate costs on benign activity. A well-designed regime can reduce uncertainty, which often helps innovation: companies know what is permitted, and insurers can price risk. The danger is capture, where large firms shape rules to keep out competitors. So I’d favour principles-based regulation, safe harbours for research, and public auditing infrastructure. That is not “light” regulation; it is regulation that targets conduct rather than attempting to control a general-purpose technology.

### Pronunciation & prosody

- **Hedging intonation:** *I suspect...* — xuống nhẹ, không lên giọng như câu hỏi.
- **Contrastive stress:** *The burden of proof should fall on the ORGANISATION, not on the PUBLIC.*
- **Connected speech:** *not at all* /nɒt‿ət‿ɔːl/, *would have* /ˈwʊd əv/.
- **Word stress:** *accountability* /əˌkaʊntəˈbɪləti/ — nhấn âm 4; *precautionary* /prɪˈkɔːʃənəri/ — nhấn âm 2.

| ❌ Sai | ✅ Đúng | Vì sao |
|---|---|---|
| *AI can do mistakes.* | *AI can make mistakes.* | *Make a mistake*. |
| *It is depend on the user.* | *It depends on the user.* | *Depend* là động từ. |
| *We should ban it totally.* | *We should restrict it to high-stakes uses.* | Tránh tuyệt đối hóa. |
| *The technology is very dangerous, I think.* | *The technology carries serious risks, particularly when...* | Cụ thể hóa, tránh cảm tính. |

## Topic 3 — Culture & Globalisation: Identity, Nuance, Comparison

**Mục tiêu:** Thảo luận văn hóa và toàn cầu hóa; tránh nhị phân *good/bad*; dùng so sánh có điều kiện; thể hiện sắc thái.

### Part 2 cue card

> Describe a tradition in your culture that has changed.
>
> You should say:
> - what the tradition is
> - how it has changed
> - why it changed
> - and explain whether you think the change is positive.

### Outline

| Phần | Nội dung |
|---|---|
| Tradition | Tet, Vietnamese New Year |
| Change | fewer fireworks, more travel, online greetings, smaller meals |
| Why | urbanisation, safety rules, cost, technology |
| Evaluation | positive in access, negative in loss of local ritual; mixed |

### Model answer

> I’d like to talk about Tet, the Vietnamese New Year, which has changed quite noticeably during my lifetime. When I was a child, the build-up was slow and local. Families would spend days cleaning the house, cooking traditional food and visiting neighbours. There were fireworks in the city centre, and the streets were unusually quiet because so many people had gone back to their hometowns.
>
> These days, some of that has shifted. Fireworks have been restricted for safety and cost reasons, which I think most people accept. More families travel during the holiday instead of staying at home, and many greetings now happen through messaging apps rather than in person. The food is still important, but smaller households mean less cooking and more buying. In a sense, Tet has become more convenient and more commercial.
>
> Is that a loss? Partly. Something is lost when a ritual becomes a transaction, and when the neighbourly visits disappear, the holiday becomes less collective. But something is gained too: people who live far from home can still participate, and the burden on women, who often did most of the cooking and cleaning, has been reduced. So I’d say the change is mixed rather than simply negative. The tradition has adapted, and the question is whether the parts that made it meaningful — family time, memory, generosity — survive the adaptation.

### Part 3 questions

1. Is globalisation a threat to local cultures?
2. Should governments protect traditional arts financially?
3. Can a tradition survive if young people stop practising it?
4. Is cultural appropriation always harmful?
5. Do museums have a duty to return cultural objects?
6. Will national cultures become less distinct in the future?

### Model answers

> **Is globalisation a threat to local cultures?**
>
> It can be, but the threat is often misdescribed. The danger is not that people suddenly stop being Vietnamese or Japanese; it’s that the infrastructure of cultural transmission — local languages, crafts, neighbourhood rituals — loses its economic and social base. A language can disappear not because it is banned but because parents decide it is not worth passing on. Globalisation accelerates that by making certain skills and languages more valuable in the labour market. But it also creates new audiences and revenues for local culture. So the outcome depends on policy: whether governments fund transmission, whether schools treat local knowledge as serious, and whether communities control how their culture is represented.

> **Is cultural appropriation always harmful?**
>
> No, but the word is doing too much work. Borrowing is a normal feature of cultural life; no tradition is pure, and exchange can be respectful and creative. The harm arises in specific conditions: when a dominant group takes a practice that is sacred or marginalised, strips it of context, and profits while the originating community is excluded or mocked. That is different from a chef adapting a recipe or a musician learning a form. So I’d resist a blanket condemnation. The relevant questions are about power, consent, attribution and whether the community benefits. Without those, “appropriation” becomes a way of policing boundaries rather than protecting people.

### Pronunciation & prosody

- **Word stress:** *globalisation* /ˌɡləʊbəlaɪˈzeɪʃn/ — nhấn âm 4; *authenticity* /ˌɔːθenˈtɪsəti/ — nhấn âm 3; *appropriation* /əˌprəʊpriˈeɪʃn/ — nhấn âm 4.
- **Contrast:** *not because it is banned, but because parents decide...* — nhấn *banned* và *parents*.
- **Listing intonation:** *family time, memory, generosity* — xuống giọng từng mục, lên nhẹ trước mục cuối.
- **Weak forms:** *would have* /ˈwʊd əv/, *have to* /ˈhæf tə/.

### Common Vietnamese mistakes

| ❌ Sai | ✅ Đúng | Vì sao |
|---|---|---|
| *Globalisation makes culture lose.* | *Globalisation can erode local culture.* | *Lose* không ngoại động từ ở đây. |
| *It is a double-edged sword, so it has both good and bad.* | *It is a double-edged sword: it expands access while weakening transmission.* | Cụ thể hóa, tránh sáo ngữ. |
| *Traditional culture is very important for us.* | *Traditional culture matters because it shapes how we understand ourselves.* | Tránh *very important* chung chung. |
| *Young people don’t like tradition.* | *Young people may lack the time, money or context to practise it.* | Tránh khái quát hóa. |

## Topic 4 — Work, Fulfilment & Language: Values, Evaluation, Experience

**Mục tiêu:** Nói về công việc và ý nghĩa; đánh giá có tiêu chí; kể trải nghiệm cá nhân mà không lan man; dùng ngôn ngữ so sánh.  
**Thời lượng:** 50 phút.

2. Is it important to enjoy your job?

### Useful language

| Cụm | Nghĩa | Ví dụ |
|---|---|---|
| a sense of purpose | cảm giác có mục đích | *Work gives many people a sense of purpose.* |
| intrinsic reward | phần thưởng nội tại | *Teaching offers intrinsic rewards.* |
| extrinsic motivation | động lực bên ngoài | *Bonuses are extrinsic motivation.* |
| burn-out | kiệt sức | *Long hours can lead to burn-out.* |
| work-life balance | cân bằng công việc–cuộc sống | *...a better work-life balance.* |
| to find something fulfilling | thấy điều gì đó ý nghĩa | *I find research fulfilling.* |
| to trade off | đánh đổi | *People trade off income for time.* |
| a means to an end | phương tiện để đạt mục đích | *For some, work is a means to an end.* |
| to derive satisfaction from | có được thỏa mãn từ | *She derives satisfaction from solving problems.* |
| to price something | định giá | *We rarely price free time.* |
| the quality of attention | chất lượng sự chú ý | *...the quality of attention we bring.* |
| linguistic relativity | thuyết tương đối ngôn ngữ | *Linguistic relativity is contested.* |
| to code-switch | chuyển mã ngôn ngữ | *I code-switch between Vietnamese and English.* |
| a different version of yourself | một phiên bản khác của bạn | *Speaking English reveals a different version of yourself.* |

### Part 2 cue card

> Describe a job you would find fulfilling, even if it paid less than another job you could do.
>
> You should say:
> - what the job is
> - what it would involve
> - why it would be fulfilling
> - and explain what you would give up for it.

### Outline

| Phần | Nội dung |
|---|---|
| Job | community librarian or adult literacy teacher |
| Involve | teaching, curating, helping people access information |
| Why | visible impact, intellectual work, relationships |
| Give up | higher salary, status; but not entirely — need enough to live |

### Model answer

> I’d like to talk about a job I think I would find genuinely fulfilling: working as an adult literacy teacher in a community centre. It’s not a glamorous profession, and in most countries it pays considerably less than the corporate work I could do with my qualifications. But it involves helping people acquire a skill that changes what they can do in practical, measurable ways.
>
> The job would involve teaching small groups, assessing what each learner needs, and building materials that are relevant to their lives. Some learners might be migrants who need English for work; others might be adults who never finished school. The work would be intellectually demanding, because you have to explain something you take for granted, and emotionally demanding, because progress can be slow and uneven.
>
> Why would it be fulfilling? Partly because the impact is visible. You can see someone read a letter from a doctor or write a job application for the first time. Partly because it involves relationships rather than transactions; you’d get to know the same people over months. And partly because it would use the skills I value most — language, patience, explanation — rather than the skills the market rewards most.
>
> What would I give up? A higher salary, certainly, and probably some status. I wouldn’t pretend that money doesn’t matter; I’d need enough to live without anxiety. But I’d be willing to trade a certain amount of income for work that I don’t dread on Sunday evening. That seems like a reasonable trade, provided I’m honest about the costs.

*≈270 từ · khoảng 1 phút 50 giây.*

### Part 3 questions

1. Why do some people stay in jobs they dislike?
2. Should employers be responsible for their employees’ well-being?
3. Is it realistic to expect work to provide meaning?
4. Does speaking a second language change your personality?
5. Should governments fund adult education?
6. Will automation make work more or less fulfilling?

### Model answers

> **Is it realistic to expect work to provide meaning?**
>
> It’s realistic to expect work not to be meaningless, but it’s risky to expect it to be the main source of meaning. Work can provide structure, mastery, colleagues and a sense of contribution; those are real goods. But when people expect all of their identity and fulfilment to come from a job, they become vulnerable to exploitation and disappointment. The relationship is also asymmetric: employers can withdraw meaning as easily as they withdraw a bonus. So I’d say work should be meaningful enough to be worth doing, but a healthy life needs sources of meaning outside it — relationships, craft, community, rest.

> **Does speaking a second language change your personality?**
>
> I think it changes your behaviour, and that can feel like a change of personality. When I speak English, I’m more direct and more willing to disagree, because that’s what the language and the contexts I’ve used it in reward. In Vietnamese, I’m more careful about hierarchy and indirectness. But I’d be cautious about the strong version of linguistic relativity — the idea that language determines thought. It’s more plausible that language gives you access to different repertoires and different social roles. You’re not a different person; you’re the same person with different tools and different expectations.

### Pronunciation & prosody

- **Word stress:** *fulfilment* /fʊlˈfɪlmənt/ — nhấn âm 2; *intrinsic* /ɪnˈtrɪnzɪk/ — nhấn âm 2; *extrinsic* /ekˈstrɪnzɪk/ — nhấn âm 2.
- **Contrastive stress:** *not the skills the MARKET rewards most, but the skills I VALUE most.*
- **Intonation for evaluation:** *That seems like a reasonable trade, provided I’m honest about the costs.* — xuống nhẹ ở *trade*, lên nhẹ ở *provided*.
- **Linking:** *would I give up* → /wʊd‿aɪ ɡɪv‿ʌp/.

### Common Vietnamese mistakes

| ❌ Sai | ✅ Đúng | Vì sao |
|---|---|---|
| *I want a job which it is meaningful.* | *I want a job that is meaningful.* | Không dùng *which it*. |
| *I prefer more free time than money.* | *I’d rather have more free time than more money.* | Cấu trúc *would rather*. |
| *Working too much is bad for health.* | *Working excessively can damage health.* | Tránh tuyệt đối hóa. |
| *It gives me a lot of meanings.* | *It gives my work a sense of meaning.* | *Meaning* không đếm được trong ngữ cảnh này. |
