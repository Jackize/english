**Level:** B1 (CEFR) · **IELTS:** khoảng 4.5–5.5 · **Số unit:** 8  
**Kỹ năng:** skim, scan, T/F/NG, Matching Headings, MCQ, Sentence/Summary Completion, Matching Information, Table Completion, Short Answer  
**Ngôn ngữ:** hướng dẫn tiếng Việt; bài đọc, câu hỏi và đáp án tiếng Anh

> Đây là level đầu tiên học **format IELTS Academic Reading**. Bài đọc dài 500–650 từ, có 10–13 câu hỏi và yêu cầu tìm **evidence** trong bài. Mục tiêu không phải làm thật nhanh, mà là làm đúng và hiểu vì sao đúng.

## 0. Giới thiệu & cách học

### 0.1 Sau 8 unit, bạn có thể

- Đọc bài 500–650 từ trong 18–22 phút.
- Làm đúng phần lớn câu T/F/NG, Matching Headings, MCQ, Sentence Completion, Summary Completion, Matching Information và Table Completion ở mức B1.
- Tìm được câu chứng minh cho mỗi đáp án.
- Phân biệt **False** và **Not Given**; phân biệt **ý chính** và **chi tiết**.
- Ghi lỗi theo 4 nhóm: từ vựng, đọc sai câu hỏi, hiểu sai paraphrase, bất cẩn.

### 0.2 Cách học một unit

1. **Đọc câu hỏi trước (2 phút).** Gạch keyword và giới hạn số từ.
2. **Skim bài (2 phút).** Đọc tiêu đề, câu đầu mỗi đoạn, tên riêng, số liệu.
3. **Làm bài (15–18 phút).** Mỗi đáp án phải có một câu chứng minh.
4. **Chấm bài (3 phút).** Đọc answer key, đánh dấu evidence.
5. **Ghi lỗi (2 phút).** Ghi 1–3 lỗi và cách sửa.
6. **Đọc lại sau 2 ngày.** Không xem đáp án.

### 0.3 Quy tắc vàng

- **Không dùng kiến thức ngoài bài.** Mọi đáp án phải nằm trong bài.
- **Không tìm từ giống hệt.** IELTS dùng paraphrase.
- **Không dừng quá 90 giây ở một câu.** Đánh dấu, quay lại sau.
- **Không bỏ trống.** Nếu hết giờ, chọn đáp án hợp lý nhất.
- **Kiểm tra số từ** ở Sentence/Summary Completion.

## Unit 1 — True/False/Not Given

**Mục tiêu:** Phân biệt True, False và Not Given; tìm evidence; nhận diện từ giới hạn.  
**Độ dài:** 520 từ · **Câu hỏi:** 10 T/F/NG · **Thời gian:** 22 phút.

### Before you read

1. Bạn có biết “community garden” là gì không?
2. Ở thành phố của bạn có khu vườn cộng đồng không?
3. Theo bạn, trồng rau trong thành phố có lợi ích gì?

### Reading passage

**Growing Together: The Rise of Community Gardens**

In many cities, empty land is becoming harder to find. Yet some residents are turning small plots of unused land into community gardens. These are shared spaces where people grow vegetables, flowers and herbs. Each member usually pays a small fee and agrees to help with watering, weeding and cleaning. The gardens are often managed by local groups rather than by the government.

Supporters say community gardens offer more than fresh food. They can improve air quality, reduce stress and bring neighbours together. In a large city, people may live close to each other for years without speaking. A garden gives them a reason to talk. Some gardens also run free classes for children, teaching them where food comes from and how to grow it.

There are economic benefits too. A family that grows its own vegetables may spend less on food, especially during the summer. In some areas, community gardens provide training and part-time work for young people. Local shops may also benefit because more people visit the area.

However, community gardens are not always easy to start. The land must be safe and legally available. Some plots have polluted soil, which means the soil must be replaced before food can be grown. Others lack water or shade. In addition, members must agree on rules about noise, pets and opening hours. Without clear rules, conflicts can appear.

Another challenge is time. Growing food requires regular care, and many people are busy with work and family. A garden can fail if only a few members do most of the work. Successful groups usually share tasks fairly and plan for the months when members are away.

Some cities now support community gardens with small grants, tools and training. Supporters argue that this money is well spent because the gardens improve public health and social contact. Critics, however, say that the same money could be used to build more homes or improve public transport. They also point out that community gardens cannot replace large farms or solve food shortages.

Most gardeners agree that the main benefit is not the amount of food produced. It is the experience of working outside, learning from others and watching something grow. As one volunteer explained, “I came for the vegetables, but I stayed for the people.”

### Questions

Do the following statements agree with the information in the passage?

Write **TRUE** if the statement agrees with the information, **FALSE** if the statement contradicts the information, and **NOT GIVEN** if there is no information on this.

1. Community gardens are usually managed by the government.
2. Some community gardens teach children about food.
3. People who grow vegetables always spend less on food than other people.
4. Community gardens can help neighbours meet each other.
5. All community gardens have safe soil.
6. Members sometimes disagree about rules.
7. Community gardens require regular work.
8. Successful gardens share tasks among members.
9. Critics believe community gardens are the best solution to food shortages.
10. The volunteer says the social side of the garden became important to her.

### Answer key

1. **FALSE** — Bài nói *“The gardens are often managed by local groups rather than by the government.”*
2. **TRUE** — *“Some gardens also run free classes for children, teaching them where food comes from...”*
3. **FALSE** — Bài nói *“may spend less”*, không phải *always*. Từ *always* làm câu sai.
4. **TRUE** — *“A garden gives them a reason to talk.”*
5. **FALSE** — *“Some plots have polluted soil”*; không phải tất cả đều an toàn.
6. **TRUE** — *“Without clear rules, conflicts can appear.”*
7. **TRUE** — *“Growing food requires regular care...”*
8. **TRUE** — *“Successful groups usually share tasks fairly...”*
9. **FALSE** — Critics nói community gardens *“cannot replace large farms or solve food shortages”*.
10. **TRUE** — *“I came for the vegetables, but I stayed for the people.”*

### Strategy — T/F/NG

1. Gạch **keyword** và **từ giới hạn**: *all, some, always, never, only, mainly*.
2. Tìm đoạn chứa thông tin, đọc cả câu.
3. So sánh **nghĩa**, không so sánh từ.
4. Nếu bài nói ngược → **False**. Nếu bài không đề cập → **Not Given**.
5. Đặc biệt cẩn thận với *may, some, often*: chúng không đồng nghĩa với *always/all*.

### Common mistakes

- Chọn **False** khi bài chỉ không nói tới → phải là **Not Given**.
- Thấy *government* trong bài và câu hỏi rồi chọn **True**, bỏ qua *rather than*.
- Bỏ qua *always* trong câu 3.
- Dùng hiểu biết ngoài bài để kết luận.

3. “Congestion charge” là gì?

### Reading passage

**How Cities Are Fighting Traffic**

**A.** For decades, city planners believed that building more roads would solve traffic problems. The idea seemed simple: if there are more lanes, cars will move faster. In practice, however, new roads often attract more drivers. This phenomenon is known as “induced demand”. A city may widen a road, only to find that within a few years it is as crowded as before.

**B.** One solution is to charge drivers for entering the busiest parts of a city. London introduced a congestion charge in 2003. Drivers must pay a daily fee to enter the central zone. The money is used to improve public transport. Supporters say the charge has reduced traffic and raised funds for buses and trains. Critics argue that it is unfair to people who cannot afford the fee or who need a car for work.

**C.** Another approach is to make public transport more attractive. This means reliable buses, frequent trains and simple ticket systems. When public transport is fast and comfortable, some drivers leave their cars at home. Cities such as Copenhagen have also built safe cycle lanes. Cycling is cheap, healthy and produces no pollution. However, it works best in flat cities with good weather and a strong cycling culture.

**D.** Some cities use technology to manage traffic. Sensors can count cars and change traffic lights in real time. Apps can guide drivers to empty parking spaces, reducing the time spent looking for a place. These systems can make existing roads more efficient, but they do not solve the basic problem of too many cars.

**E.** Changing behaviour is perhaps the hardest part. People are used to their cars, especially in areas with poor public transport. Governments can encourage change through taxes, parking fees and car-free zones. They can also make walking and cycling safer. But policies are more likely to succeed when people have practical alternatives. A ban without a good bus service will simply make life harder.

**F.** There is no single solution. The most successful cities combine several measures: better public transport, safe walking and cycling routes, clear pricing and careful planning. They also listen to residents and adjust policies over time. Reducing traffic is not only a technical problem; it is also a political and social one.

### Strategy — Matching Headings

1. Đọc headings, gạch ý chính.
2. Đọc **câu đầu + câu cuối** mỗi đoạn.
3. Tìm ý chung, không chọn vì một từ khóa.
4. Loại heading quá hẹp (chỉ một chi tiết) hoặc quá rộng.
5. Làm đoạn dễ trước; đánh dấu heading đã dùng.

### Common mistakes

- Chọn heading mô tả ví dụ thay vì ý chính.
- Bỏ qua câu cuối đoạn, nơi thường chứa kết luận.
- Dành quá nhiều thời gian cho một đoạn khó.

### Reading passage

**Why Sleep Matters**

Sleep is often treated as optional. Students stay up late to study, workers answer emails at midnight, and many people use their phones in bed. Yet scientists agree that sleep is essential for both body and mind. Adults generally need seven to nine hours a night, although the exact amount varies from person to person.

During sleep, the brain does not simply switch off. It processes information from the day, stores important memories and clears waste products. Studies suggest that people who sleep well are better at learning new skills and remembering facts. Sleep also affects mood: a poor night’s sleep can make people feel irritable, anxious or less able to concentrate.

The body repairs itself during sleep too. Growth and tissue repair happen mostly at night. The immune system becomes stronger, helping the body fight infections. Lack of sleep, on the other hand, is linked to weight gain, heart problems and a higher risk of accidents. Drivers who are very tired may react more slowly than usual, even if they do not feel sleepy.

Many people try to catch up at weekends, but this is not a perfect solution. Sleeping late on Sunday can disturb the body clock, making Monday morning harder. A regular routine is more effective: going to bed and waking up at similar times each day, including weekends. Bright light in the morning helps the body clock, while dark, quiet rooms help sleep at night.

Technology is part of the problem. The blue light from screens can trick the brain into thinking it is still daytime. Scrolling through messages can also keep the mind active. Experts recommend putting phones away at least half an hour before bed. Reading a paper book, taking a warm shower or listening to calm music may help instead.

Some people use sleeping pills, but doctors usually advise caution. Pills can be useful for short periods, but they do not solve the underlying cause. They may also become less effective over time. For most people, better habits are safer and more lasting.

Sleep is not a luxury. It is a basic need, as important as food and exercise. Small changes — a regular bedtime, less screen time and a darker room — can make a real difference.

### Reading passage

**Learning a Language as an Adult**

Many adults believe that language learning is only easy for children. They remember how quickly young children pick up new words and assume that the ability disappears with age. Research tells a more complex story. Children may learn accents more naturally, but adults have advantages too: they understand grammar, can organise their study and are often more motivated.

One advantage is that adults can use strategies. They can make vocabulary lists, review them regularly and look for patterns. They can also choose materials that interest them, such as podcasts, films or news articles. This makes learning more enjoyable and helps them remember new language.

However, adults often have less time. Work, family and other responsibilities can make regular study difficult. Short, frequent sessions are usually better than one long session a week. Twenty minutes a day is more effective than three hours on Sunday. The key is consistency, not intensity.

Another challenge is fear. Adults may feel embarrassed when they make mistakes. Children are often less worried about looking silly. This fear can stop adults from speaking, which slows progress. Teachers often advise learners to accept mistakes as part of the process and to practise in a supportive environment.

Pronunciation is another area where adults may need extra help. Some sounds do not exist in the first language, and the mouth may need time to learn new movements. Regular listening and imitation can help. Recording yourself and comparing your speech with a model is also useful.

Technology has made practice easier. Language apps, online tutors and video calls allow learners to study at home. However, technology cannot replace real communication. Learners still need to speak with other people, ask questions and receive feedback.

Finally, motivation matters. Adults who have a clear reason for learning — a job, travel, family or personal interest — are more likely to continue. Goals should be specific and realistic. Instead of “I want to be fluent”, a learner might aim to “have a five-minute conversation about my work” or “read one short article a day”.

Language learning is not a race. Progress may be slow, and plateaus are normal. Adults who combine regular practice, useful strategies and real communication can make excellent progress, even if they started later in life.

**Managing Water in the City**

When rain falls in a city, it often cannot soak into the ground. Roads, roofs and pavements are covered with concrete, so water runs quickly into drains. In heavy storms, the drains may overflow, causing floods. Polluted water can also flow into rivers. City planners are therefore looking for ways to slow water down and let it soak into the ground.

One solution is the rain garden. This is a shallow area planted with grass, flowers and small trees. It collects water from roofs or roads and allows it to soak into the soil. Rain gardens are usually built near buildings or car parks. They need little maintenance after the first year, although young plants must be watered during dry periods. They also look attractive and can support insects and birds.

Another option is the green roof. Plants are grown on top of a building, often in a special layer of soil. Green roofs absorb rain, reduce heat and improve air quality. They can also save energy because they keep buildings cooler in summer. However, they are heavier than normal roofs, so the building must be strong enough. They also cost more to install.

Permeable paving is a third approach. Instead of solid concrete, the surface has small gaps that let water pass through. It is useful for car parks, paths and quiet roads. It can reduce flooding and allow water to reach the soil. However, it may not work well in areas with heavy traffic or very muddy ground.

Finally, storage tanks can collect rainwater from roofs. The water can be used for gardens or cleaning, reducing the demand for treated water. Tanks must be covered to prevent mosquitoes and dirt. In some cities, new buildings are required to include them.

These methods work best when they are used together. A single rain garden will not solve a city’s flooding problem, but many small projects can make a difference. Planning is important: cities need to know where water collects and which areas are most at risk. Community support also matters, because residents must care for the new spaces.

The future of remote work will probably not be all-or-nothing. Different jobs, industries and individuals will find different solutions.
